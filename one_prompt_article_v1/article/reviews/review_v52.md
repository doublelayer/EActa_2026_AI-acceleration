P00006  Abstract
P00007  Accelerating catalyst discovery with artificial intelligence (AI) requires accurate density functional theory (DFT) calculations, useful machine learning (ML) predictions, and clear scientific writing. We demonstrate this with the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) on molecular dual-atom site catalysts (DASCs). DFT calculations for 8[[INSERT id=0; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:00:45Z: 8]][[DELETE id=0; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:00:45Z: 7]] molecular catalysts established ORR adsorption energetics. Ridge regression models trained on these computational results predicted adsorption potential energies for 1[[INSERT id=1; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:00:58Z: 260]][[DELETE id=1; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:00:58Z: 347]] optimized molecular catalysts, which enabled their ranking. In the lowest predicted overpotential decile, iron (Fe) adsorption centers predominated, while sulfur (S) bridge atoms and 1,2-phenylenediamine were enriched relative to the full predicted set. We then guided a large language model (LLM) to write this whole article using verified computational results, ML predictions, three databases, literature supplied as portable document format files, and figures. This case study illustrates [[DELETE id=2; author=OpenAI Codex; date=2026-10-16T00:44:00Z: how]][[INSERT id=2; author=OpenAI Codex; date=2026-10-16T00:44:00Z: two ways in which]] AI can accelerate catalyst discovery[[DELETE id=3; author=OpenAI Codex; date=2026-10-16T00:44:00Z:  through,]][[INSERT id=3; author=OpenAI Codex; date=2026-10-16T00:44:00Z: :]] first, ML-based screening[[DELETE id=4; author=OpenAI Codex; date=2026-10-16T00:44:00Z:  and,]][[INSERT id=4; author=OpenAI Codex; date=2026-10-16T00:44:00Z: ;]] second, LLM assisted scientific writing.

P00022  2. Computational methods
P00023  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00024  The molecular catalysts were assembled from the components shown in Figure 2. Our previous data article defined B1 as the 1,2-phenylenediamine fragment and B2 as a 1,8-naphthalenediamine fragment [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. A2 is 2-hydroxyisophthalaldehyde fragment. Each molecule contains one dual-atom site with two metal-atom adsorption centers. The two metal positions were occupied by Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, or Cu, and each of the two bridge positions contained N, O, or S. Every clean and adsorbate structure in the DFT analysis was fully optimized. Three linked molecular catalyst databases were used: the published structure database, the fixed DFT dataset of 8[[INSERT id=46; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:24Z: 8]][[DELETE id=46; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:24Z: 7]] catalysts, and the larger dataset of 1[[INSERT id=47; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:29Z: 259]][[DELETE id=47; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:29Z: 347]] optimized catalysts used for ML predictions. Each optimized structure and its energy were stored in an Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database with the catalyst name, metal identities, ligand components, and charge metadata. The ads_site field identifies the metal-atom adsorption center occupied by each intermediate.
P00025  2.2. DFT calculations and adsorption structures
P00026  Spin polarized DFT calculations were performed with GPAW 2[[INSERT id=48; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:47Z: 4]][[DELETE id=48; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:47Z: 5]].[[INSERT id=49; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:48Z: 6]][[DELETE id=49; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:01:48Z: 7]] and ASE 3.28.0 software [10.1063/5.0182685; 10.1088/1361-648X/aa680e]. The projector augmented wave method, revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and D4 dispersion correction were used [10.1063/1.5090222]. A plane wave cutoff of 550 eV, real space grid spacing of 0.1[[INSERT id=50; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:02:27Z: 5]][[DELETE id=50; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:02:27Z: 6]] Å, and 6.00 Å of vacuum were applied. Each atomic structure was optimized until the maximum force fell below 0.05 eV Å⁻¹, and only calculations that converged with these parameters entered the fixed DFT set. The setup follows earlier work on related molecular catalysts [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. The total charge was set to +2 for every modeled catalyst, including N bridged structures. This consistent cohort represents one charge assignment and the spin states reached by the spin polarized optimizations. A broader electronic structure survey should compare alternative charges, mixed oxidation states, and spin states.
P00027  O, OH, and OOH were placed at either metal-atom adsorption center in the active site , and each structure was fully optimized. Gas phase H₂ and H₂O reference structures were calculated with the same settings. Total energies were matched by catalyst name and `ads_site`, so each reaction pathway contains values for the same molecular structure and adsorption center.
P00028  Adsorption energies for O, OH, and OOH were calculated from the total energies of the corresponding adsorbed structure, clean catalyst, H₂, and H₂O:
P00029  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OH} = E_{*OH} + (1/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(1)
P00030  			[[MATH: ΔE_{O} = E_{*O} + E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(2)
P00031  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OOH} = E_{*OOH} + (3/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - 2E_{H_{2}O}]]	(3)
P00032  In Eqs 1–3, the left side is the adsorption potential energy of the named intermediate. The terms on the right are the electronic total energies of the clean and adsorbed catalysts and the gas-phase references; * marks the selected adsorption center. 
P00033  2.3. Adsorption free energies and ORR thermodynamics
P00034  Adsorption potential energies were converted to adsorption free energies by adding combined zero point energy and entropy corrections and an empirical solvation contribution:
P00035  			[[MATH: ΔG_{X} = ΔE_{X} + ΔE_{ZPE,X} - TΔS_{X} + ΔG_{solv,X}]]	(4)
P00036  In Eq 4, X denotes O, OH, or OOH; E and G denote potential energy and Gibbs free energy; T is temperature; S is entropy; ZPE identifies the zero point energy correction; solv identifies solvation. The adopted combined zero point energy and entropy corrections were 0.35 eV for OH, 0.05 eV for O, and 0.40 eV for OOH at 300 K, using the tabulated reference convention and the additive O + OH approximation for OOH [10.1016/j.chemphys.2005.05.038]. Solvation corrections of −0.30 eV were applied to OH and OOH, corresponding to two hydrogen bonds with an estimated stabilization of 0.15 eV each; no solvation correction was applied to O [10.1039/c1cp21228a]. The resulting net additions were 0.05, 0.05, and 0.10 eV for OH, O, and OOH, respectively. These rounded combined corrections were applied consistently to the DFT and ML adsorption energies. The adopted reaction corrections include gas-reference contributions. The four reduction potentials were evaluated with the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) method [10.1021/jp047349j]:
P00037  			[[MATH: U_{1} = (4.92 eV - ΔG_{OOH}) / e; U_{2} = (ΔG_{OOH} - ΔG_{O}) / e; U_{3} = (ΔG_{O} - ΔG_{OH}) / e; U_{4} = ΔG_{OH} / e]]	(5)
P00038  Equation 5 defines the positive reduction potentials for the four steps O₂ to OOH, OOH to O, O to OH, and OH to H₂O; * identifies an adsorbed intermediate and e is the elementary charge. The limiting potential is defined as the lowest of the four step potentials:
P00039  			[[MATH: U_{L} = min(U_{1}, U_{2}, U_{3}, U_{4})]]	(6)
P00040  The symbol on the left of Eq 6 denotes the limiting potential. The ORR overpotential was evaluated relative to the equilibrium potential of 1.23 V:
P00041  			[[MATH: η_{ORR} = 1.23 V - U_{L}]]	(7)
P00042  Equation 7 defines the ORR overpotential. Chemical subscripts in Eqs 5–7 identify the adsorbed intermediate or reaction quantity.
P00043  2.4. Scaling and thermodynamic activity analyses
P00044  The relations of O and OOH adsorption free energies to OH adsorption free energy were evaluated by ordinary least squares:
P00045  			[[MATH: ΔG_{O} = α_{O} ΔG_{OH} + β_{O}; ΔG_{OOH} = α_{OOH} ΔG_{OH} + β_{OOH}]]	(8)
P00046  In Eq 8, α and β are the slope and intercept for the intermediate identified by the subscript. The fitted relations were propagated through the four ORR steps to obtain the scaling based activity curve in Figures 3 and 4. An ideal reference relation was defined with an OOH–OH free energy separation of 2.46 eV:
P00047  			[[MATH: ΔG_{OOH}^{ideal} = ΔG_{OH} + 2.46 eV; η_{ideal} = 1.23 V - min(ΔG_{OH} / e, (2.46 eV - ΔG_{OH}) / e)]]	(9)
P00048  In Eq 9, OH removal defines the left branch and OOH formation defines the right branch. The branches meet at an OH adsorption free energy of 1.23 eV and zero overpotential. Catalyst activity was interpreted thermodynamically; kinetic activity also depends on activation barriers.
P00049  2.5. Regression analysis and catalyst family screening
P00050  Of the 8[[INSERT id=51; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:09Z: 8]][[DELETE id=51; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:09Z: 7]] DFT catalyst structures, [[INSERT id=52; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:12Z: 75]][[DELETE id=52; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:12Z: 50]] were selected for model training and 1[[INSERT id=53; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:16Z: 5]][[DELETE id=53; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:16Z: 0]] for validation. Separate ridge regression models were fitted for OH, O, and OOH. Descriptors of the clean catalysts represented the electron population, charge, spin, metal pair polarization, and ligand environment. Eight predictors were used for OH and O, and six were used for OOH. The parameter tables retain the training medians, means, standard deviations, standardized coefficients, and ridge parameters. The ridge parameter was 0.10 for OH and 1.00 for O and OOH. The models were applied to the separate database of 1[[INSERT id=54; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:22Z: 260]][[DELETE id=54; author=Ba Long Nguyen; date=2026-10-16T03:05:22Z: 347]] optimized structures described in Section 2.1.
P00051  The predicted OH, O, and OOH values were joined by catalyst name and `ads_site` and converted from adsorption potential energies to free energies with the same corrections in Section 2.3 before the CHE step potentials and overpotentials were evaluated. Complete catalyst and adsorption center combinations were selected before the stated energy filters were applied to the predictions. For comparison of structural families, the most active pool in each DFT or ML dataset was defined as the lowest 10% of retained overpotentials, rounded upward to a whole catalyst and adsorption center combination.

P00073  3.4. Molecular catalyst design in context
P00074  Our previous research on the studied molecular catalyst family found that phenylene and naphthalene diamines used in synthesis alter the distance between Co centers, oxygen intermediate binding geometry, and ORR selectivity [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. Other authors speculate on  bidentate OOH binding (which we do not see in our DFT calculations) and changes in metal coordination with bridge and ligand structure [10.1021/jacs.4c12705; 10.1021/acscatal.6c00046].  Herewith, our earlier Co–Co study explored a related O-bridged molecular catalyst at a total charge of +4, outside the +2 charge set screened here. Its DFT analysis suggested a lower ORR overpotential than those found in the present set, although that study did not apply the same empirical free-energy corrections used here . The values therefore illustrate the scope for further exploration rather than a directly comparable ranking. That study and Sun's Co–Co complex show that specific OOH binding geometries can give an OOH–OH free-energy separation below 3 eV; such local behavior does not describe the global fit of the present dataset .
P00075  Zhang et al. provide the closest precedent through catalysis-unit engineering [10.1002/adma.202403549]. They evaluated 60 homonuclear salphen units assembled from ten metals and six coordination environments, calculated oxygen evolution reaction (OER) free energies and scaling relations with DFT, ranked 27 descriptors with XGBoost, and used the Sure Independence Screening and Sparsifying Operator (SISSO) to build an interpretable model before incorporating selected Ni and Co units into salphen covalent organic materials. Our ORR study covers 87 molecular catalysts in the DFT stage, includes homo- and heteronuclear metal pairs, resolves adsorption at either metal center, and extends prediction to 1347 optimized structures. The reactions and regression models differ, yet both studies show how DFT results can train ML models to identify structural trends across catalyst families.
P00076  Zhang et al. OOH–OH scaling relation has a slope of 0.96 and an intercept of 2.98 eV . The corresponding fit across our filtered ORR pathways has a slope of 0.69 and an intercept of 3.05 eV. Zhang et al. included additional metals resulting in a wider OH adsorption energy range, which  gives the regression more leverage at strong adsorption, while differences in reaction, catalyst selection, and free-energy treatment also affect a direct comparison. Both studies show that a fitted slope must be read together with the chemical domain from which it was obtained.
P00077  That division is also visible in recent screening of extended phthalocyanine dual-atom catalysts, where 81 DFT structures supported predictions for 360 additional metal pairs and identified Fe as an adsorption anchor whose neighboring metal regulates activity [10.1002/adma.74833]. In our data, DFT establishes the favorable S bridged examples, and ML carries the metal and ligand comparison across an order of magnitude more molecular structures. The regression screen therefore substitutes fast predictions for many preliminary adsorption calculations; targeted DFT and experiment can then concentrate on the families selected from that screen. This is the specific way in which ML accelerates catalyst discovery in the present study.

P00079  3.5. Alternative activity descriptors and reusable DFT data
P00080  The same DFT calculations, matched by catalyst and adsorption center, support several thermodynamic analyses of ORR.
