P00001  Accelerating Catalyst Discovery and Design with Artificial Intelligence: Molecular Dual-Atom Site Catalysts as a Case Study
P00002  Bruno Inácio da Silva Roux Leite,1 Denis Savchenko,1 Timmo-Henrik Pukk,1 Ba Long Nguyen,1 Vladislav Ivanistsev,2† Nadezda Kongi,1†
P00003  1 Institute of Chemistry, University of Tartu, Ravila 14a, 50411 Tartu, Estonia
P00004  2 Department of Chemistry, University of Latvia, Jelgavas iela 1, Riga, LV-1004, Latvia
P00005  Corresponding authors: nadezda.kongi@ut.ee; vladislav.ivanistsev@lu.lv
P00006  Abstract
P00007  Artificial intelligence can accelerate catalyst research through both prediction and scientific writing. We examine these roles using oxygen reduction on molecular dual-atom site catalysts. We analyze adsorption free energy scaling and reaction thermodynamics from density functional theory, then apply a simpler ridge regression analysis to predict the same quantities across the molecular library. Both analyses show systematic changes with the metal at the adsorption site and identify candidates near the thermodynamic activity maximum. The regression extends the comparison to sites with available clean catalyst descriptors. Artificial intelligence generated the complete manuscript text from the research record under the guidance of the researchers. This case illustrates how reusable data can support rapid analysis and article preparation while human judgment remains essential for selecting methods, interpreting results, and verifying claims.
P00008  Keywords: artificial intelligence; computational hydrogen electrode; density functional theory; machine learning; oxygen reduction reaction; molecular dual-atom site catalysts
P00009  1. Introduction
P00010  Catalyst discovery combines calculations that resolve candidate structures with decisions about which candidates to examine next. Density functional theory provides adsorption energies for defined structures, while regression models summarize patterns across a related catalyst set [1,2] [3]. Artificial intelligence can also generate a research article from such records. This raises a practical question: can rapid article generation remain reliable when structural identity, methods, data, and citations are explicit and verifiable?
P00011  We use the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) to examine this problem. The four electron pathway depends on the relative stabilization of oxygen intermediates, whose correlated adsorption free energies constrain thermodynamic activity estimates [4]. Molecular dual-atom site catalysts provide a chemically defined set in which metal identity, bridge, diamine, and adsorption site can be varied. Studies of related molecular catalysts show that binding geometry and the second metal can alter oxygen intermediate energetics [5,6]. A valid comparison therefore keeps each adsorbate value tied to the same catalyst and adsorption site.
P00012  Scientific editors have argued that a coherent theme and carefully selected figures help readers follow the question, evidence, and interpretation [7,8] [9,10]. Yet a polished story can hide conflicting evidence or uncertainty when authors select observations to fit a narrative [11]. The relevant test is whether AI can produce a coherent account while keeping claims traceable to data, methods, and sources.
P00013  Here, we examine that question using site matched adsorption records for a molecular catalyst library. Figure 1 defines the catalyst structure, Figure 2 presents adsorption scaling and oxygen reduction thermodynamics from DFT, and Figure 3 presents the corresponding quantities from a simpler ridge regression analysis. We discuss the Calle-Vallejo and Exner descriptors as examples of how reusable data support alternative interpretations. The entire manuscript text was generated by AI from materials supplied by the researchers. Human researchers guided the analysis and checked the evidence and its interpretation.
P00014  2. Computational methods
P00015  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00016  The molecular catalyst library was assembled from two metal centers, a fixed A2 dialdehyde, a B1 or B2 diamine, and two N, O, or S bridge atoms. The current clean catalyst database contains 88 structures with Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, or Cu at the two metal positions. The parent structures were generated by modular assembly and spin polarized GFN2-xTB optimization; selected DFT optimized structures were reused as templates, with one metal substituted at a time. An xTB optimized geometry was used when a suitable DFT template was unavailable [12].
P00017  We retained the catalyst identity, metal positions, ligand components, and charge metadata in the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database.
P00018  2.2. Density functional theory calculations
P00019  We performed spin polarized DFT calculations with the GPAW and ASE framework [13,14]. The calculations used the projector augmented wave method, the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and the D4 dispersion correction [15]. A plane wave basis with a 550 eV kinetic energy cutoff and a real space grid spacing of 0.16 Å were used. Molecules were placed in isolated cells with 6.00 Å vacuum and Γ point sampling. We relaxed the atomic positions and converged the electronic density self consistently. Gas phase H₂ and H₂O reference molecules were calculated with the same settings. This setup follows the GPAW methodology used for related molecular catalysts [6].
P00020  2.3. Adsorption structures and database matching
P00021  We optimized O, OH, and OOH on each available metal atom. Two initial OOH geometries, OOH-x and OOH-z, were tested. We joined records by the exact pair (catalyst name, adsorption site label), selected the lower total energy OOH orientation separately for each pair, and retained its orientation label. A pathway was accepted only when its reconstructed values agreed with the corresponding raw database records within 10⁻⁸ eV. The observation unit throughout the analysis was one catalyst and one adsorption site combination.
P00022  We defined adsorption energies from the total energies of the clean catalyst, adsorbed structures, and gas phase references using Eqs. (1)–(3). An asterisk denotes the clean adsorption site or a bound intermediate, as appropriate. The analysis used the supplied corrected adsorption free energies directly for scaling, filtering, and thermodynamic activity estimates.

P00033  2.5. Scaling and thermodynamic activity analyses
P00034  We fitted O–OH and OOH–OH adsorption free energy scaling relations by ordinary least squares using Eq. (8). We retained sites with OH adsorption free energy of at least 0.00 eV and O adsorption free energy no greater than 4.92 eV. The curves in Figures 2 and 3 propagate the corresponding global scaling fits through all four CHE steps. The electrochemical step symmetry index and free energy span offer complementary thermodynamic interpretations of the same adsorption records [16,17].
P00035  The dotted ideal reference in Eq. (9) assumes an OOH–OH free energy separation of 2.46 eV. OH removal defines its left branch and OOH formation its right branch. The branches meet at OH adsorption free energy 1.23 eV and zero overpotential. This reference illustrates balanced binding; the catalyst points and fitted volcano curves use all four reaction steps. Thermodynamic rankings describe reaction free energies, while kinetic activity also depends on activation barriers.

P00038  2.6. Simpler regression analysis
P00039  We used separate ridge regression models for OH, O, and OOH adsorption free energies. The models use clean catalyst descriptors of Bader charge and electron population, local spin, and ligand charge distribution. The supplied models contain eight predictors for OH and O and six for OOH. We applied their saved training medians, means, standard deviations, and coefficients to all available catalyst and adsorption site records. We joined the three predictions at each site and evaluated the overpotential with Eqs. (5)–(7). The same energy filters were applied to the predicted pathways. This simpler analysis is distinct from linear regression with second-order polynomial features.
P00040  Figure 3 includes every available site that passes these filters, including sites used for model fitting. It describes predicted trends across the library. We used the supplied validation predictions to check reproduction of the saved models; the all-site figure itself does not measure independent predictive accuracy.

P00043  3. Results and discussion
P00044  3.1. Molecular design space and site identity
P00045  Figure 1 shows the molecular scaffold and its modular components. The two metal positions use Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, or Cu; the bridge is N, O, or S; and the diamine is B1 or B2. The dialdehyde is fixed as A2. In each molecular catalyst, an adsorption site is a metal atom within the organic ligand framework at which an ORR intermediate is bound. The two metal atoms therefore define separate observations when both have adsorption data.

P00049  3.2. Adsorption scaling and calculated ORR thermodynamics
P00050  Figure 2a compares O and OOH adsorption free energies with OH adsorption free energy. The OOH–OH relation has a slope of 0.69 and an intercept of 2.98 eV; the O–OH relation has a slope of 1.66 and an intercept of 1.20 eV. The adsorption site families occupy different parts of these trends, with scatter around the global relations. Related molecular catalysts can alter OOH binding geometry and adsorption scaling through their paired metal centers [5,2]. The present trends provide a basis for examining such structural effects within a common molecular scaffold.

P00053  Figure 2b shows the calculated conventional computational hydrogen electrode overpotential, with the vertical axis reversed. The lowest value in the filtered working dataset is 0.34 V at the Mn adsorption site of a Cr–Mn catalyst with an S–S bridge and B1 diamine. Its stored adsorption quantities are 0.89 eV for OH, 1.99 eV for O, and 3.41 eV for OOH; the OH to water step determines the limiting potential. This thermodynamic ranking applies to the present structures, corrections, matching rules, and filters. The curve propagates the global scaling fits through the four step reaction model, as described in the Figure 2 caption.
P00054  3.3. Scaling and activity from regression predictions
P00055  Figure 3 presents the scaling relations and oxygen reduction volcano obtained from the simpler regression models. Complete predictions were available for 148 sites across 88 catalysts; the filters retained 144 sites across 87 catalysts. The predicted OOH–OH slope and intercept are 0.67 and 2.98 eV, while those for O–OH are 1.73 and 1.14 eV. These trends resemble those obtained directly from the adsorption calculations in Figure 2. The regression gives smoother trends across the metal families, as expected when a small set of descriptors summarizes the variation in adsorption free energies.
P00056  The lowest predicted overpotentials occur near the crossover between strong and weak binding. In this dataset, the lowest value is 0.30 V at the Fe adsorption site of an Fe–Mn catalyst with S bridge atoms and B1 diamine. Figure 2 instead places its lowest DFT derived overpotential at a Mn site. This difference makes the ranking useful for selecting calculations that examine whether the descriptors preserve the relevant distinctions among candidate sites. The all-site predictions describe the represented library; transfer to new chemical families requires a separate evaluation.

P00059  3.4. Reanalysis from reusable data
P00060  The exact, site matched adsorption records support the conventional CHE calculation and the alternative Calle-Vallejo and Exner analyses. With catalyst identity, adsorption site, OOH orientation, and energy convention retained, researchers can recalculate these descriptors from stored energies without repeating DFT. Changes to molecular geometry, charge state, or adsorption structure require new calculations.
P00061  The planned release will include catalyst structures and results as an Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database and machine readable JSON, together with code for reconstructing the joins and figures. These formats support findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable data practices [18]. A documented schema lets a language model prepare an alternative analysis or plot at low computational cost; researchers can then check its inputs, equations, code, and results before interpretation.
P00062  The authors report that prompt development took about two weeks and generation of the integrated draft took less than one hour. They recall comparable manual writing efforts that took months. These time figures are project specific and qualitative; comparison with human writing requires a matched study.
P00063  4. Conclusions
P00064  The adsorption calculations reveal distinct scaling trends for atomic oxygen and hydroperoxyl and show how binding strength constrains oxygen reduction thermodynamics within this molecular catalyst library. The simpler regression reproduces the broad trends and extends the comparison to sites with available clean catalyst descriptors. Differences between the calculated and predicted rankings identify candidates for further electronic structure calculations. These results support candidate prioritization within the represented chemical space.
P00065  Artificial intelligence generated the research narrative from the supplied calculations, data, figures, and literature under the guidance of the researchers. Reusable records allowed the same structural information to support direct thermodynamic analysis, regression predictions, and alternative activity descriptors. The case illustrates how rapid article preparation can place greater emphasis on the quality of the underlying research: explicit methods, traceable data, and human judgment about what the results establish.
