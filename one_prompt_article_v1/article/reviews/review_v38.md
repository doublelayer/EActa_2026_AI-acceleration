P00001  Accelerating Catalyst Discovery with Artificial Intelligence: Molecular Dual-Atom Site Catalysts as a Case Study
P00002  Bruno Inácio da Silva Roux Leite,1 Denis Savchenko,1 Timmo-Henrik Pukk,1 Ba Long Nguyen,1 Vladislav Ivanistsev,2† Nadezda Kongi,1†
P00003  1 Institute of Chemistry, University of Tartu, Ravila 14a, 50411 Tartu, Estonia
P00004  2 Department of Chemistry, University of Latvia, Jelgavas iela 1, Riga, LV-1004, Latvia
P00005  Corresponding authors: vladislav.ivanistsev@lu.lv; nadezda.kongi@ut.ee
P00006  Abstract
P00007  Artificial intelligence (AI) can accelerate catalyst discovery by shortening the route from verified results to reusable scientific communication. We test this proposition with molecular dual-atom site catalysts (DASCs) for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). Human researchers used density functional theory (DFT) to calculate adsorption energetics for 88 molecular catalysts. These calculations form the training source for a machine learning (ML) regression model designed to screen a separate database of 1176 optimized catalysts and prioritize structures for further calculation or experiment. We also developed a large language model (LLM) writing procedure through iterative scientific, editorial, and technical guidance. The procedure consolidates verified data, analyses, literature, figures, and writing rules into one final prompt for complete manuscript generation. The catalyst calculations and predictions constitute genuine electrochemical research; the article itself tests whether their communication can be delegated. Human researchers choose the questions and methods, interpret and verify the results, and remain responsible for the article. This division can reduce the delay between obtaining reliable results and making them available for reuse.
P00008  Keywords: AI; DFT; ML; LLM; oxygen reduction reaction; molecular dual-atom site catalysts
P00009  1. Introduction
P00010  Accelerating catalyst discovery with AI means shortening the interval between a scientific question and the next informed decision. DFT and ML can reduce the time required to evaluate candidates, yet a result cannot guide another calculation or experiment until other researchers can inspect and reuse it. We therefore examine scientific writing as part of catalyst discovery: communication determines when verified results become available to the scientific community.
P00011  Publication can add months between submission and the appearance of a scientific result. Bibliometric studies reported chemistry submission-to-publication intervals of approximately nine months across a journal sample and a median of 154 days for chemistry articles on the PLOS platform [10.1016/j.joi.2013.09.001; 10.1007/s11192-023-04640-6]. These intervals begin at submission. They exclude preparation before the first submission, time spent at journals that reject a manuscript, and part of the subsequent rewriting. LLM generation cannot remove editorial, reviewer, or production delays, but it can address manuscript preparation and revision.
P00012  Scientific publishing treats the coherent story as a measure of a successful paper. Editors describe story as the organizing principle of an article and give figures a central role in making a result intelligible [10.1038/nphys724; 10.1021/acsenergylett.8b02322; 10.1021/acsenergylett.1c02401; 10.1038/s41551-018-0202-5]. We agree that readers need a clear account. We question why the release of useful results should depend so strongly on the time and specialized skill required to compose that account.
P00013  Scientific data and scientific narratives are different products. No research group can test every question that its data may later answer. Machine readable data let other researchers combine results, evaluate alternative descriptors, and pursue hypotheses that the original authors did not anticipate [10.1038/sdata.2016.18]. The value of a calculation should therefore extend beyond the narrative selected for its first publication.
P00014  LLMs can close part of the resulting communication gap, provided that fluent text remains answerable to evidence. Storytelling can conceal an inconvenient result even without generative AI [10.1038/nmeth.2726], while changes in published abstract vocabulary indicate that LLM-assisted writing is already widespread [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813]. Ifargan and colleagues showed that interacting LLM agents can produce human verifiable papers from annotated data, while human guidance becomes more important as the research question grows more complex [10.1056/aioa2400555]. Their work establishes the closest precedent; our test focuses on reproducible generation of the complete article from one developed prompt.
P00015  We examine this proposition through ORR on molecular DASCs. Recent studies show how DFT and ML can screen ORR catalysts and resolve cooperation between metal atoms in dual-atom catalysts [10.1021/acscatal.6c02383; 10.1002/adma.74833]. Figure 1 defines the molecular variation. Figure 2 reports adsorption free energy scaling and calculated thermodynamic activity from the fixed DFT set of 88 catalysts. The regression analysis in Figure 3 tests prediction within the represented molecular library, and the final model will extend the screening to a separate database of 1176 optimized catalysts. Our final objective is to consolidate the verified scientific inputs and tested writing instructions into one prompt that generates the complete article for human verification.
P00016  2. Computational methods
P00017  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00018  We assembled the molecular catalysts from the components shown in Figure 1. Following the notation introduced in our data article [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806], A2 denotes the fixed dialdehyde and B1 and B2 denote the two diamines. Each molecule contains two metal centers chosen from Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu and bridge atoms selected from N, O, or S. The same article describes the molecular assembly and initial structure preparation. We fully optimized all structures used in the DFT analysis, including the clean catalysts and structures containing adsorbed intermediates. The clean database used here contains 88 molecular catalysts.
P00019  The Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database stores each optimized structure and energy with its catalyst name, metal positions, ligand components, and charge metadata. For adsorbed structures, the literal database field ads_site identifies which metal-atom adsorption center carries the intermediate.
P00020  2.2. DFT calculations and adsorption structures
P00021  We performed spin polarized DFT calculations with GPAW 26.7 and ASE 3.29.0 software [10.1063/5.0182685; 10.1088/1361-648X/aa680e]. We used the projector augmented wave method, the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and the D4 dispersion correction [10.1063/1.5090222]. The calculations used a plane wave cutoff of 550 eV, a real space grid spacing of 0.16 Å, and 6.00 Å vacuum. We fully optimized every atomic structure until the maximum force was below 0.05 eV Å⁻¹. We previously used the same calculation setup for related molecular catalysts [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1].
P00022
P00023  We placed O, OH, and OOH at either of the two metal-atom adsorption centers in the dual-atom site and fully optimized each structure. We calculated gas phase H₂ and H₂O with the same settings. We matched all total energies by catalyst name and the ads_site center label, so every reaction pathway refers to the same center of the same molecular catalyst.
P00024  We obtained the adsorption energies of O, OH, and OOH from the total energies of the corresponding adsorbed structure, clean catalyst, H₂, and H₂O:
P00025  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OH} = E_{*OH} + (1/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(1)
P00026  			[[MATH: ΔE_{O} = E_{*O} + E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(2)
P00027  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OOH} = E_{*OOH} + (3/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - 2E_{H_{2}O}]]	(3)
P00028  In Eqs. (1)–(3), the left side is the adsorption energy of the named intermediate. The terms on the right are the total energies of the adsorbed catalyst, the clean catalyst, gas phase H₂, and gas phase H₂O; * denotes the chosen adsorption center. We used the supplied corrected adsorption free energies for scaling and thermodynamic analyses.
P00029  2.3. Adsorption free energies and ORR thermodynamics
P00030  We converted adsorption energies to adsorption free energies by adding the zero point and entropic corrections:
P00031  			[[MATH: ΔG_{X} = ΔE_{X} + ΔE_{ZP,X} - TΔS_{X}]]	(4)
P00032  In Eq. (4), X denotes O, OH, or OOH; E and G denote electronic energy and Gibbs free energy; T is temperature; S is entropy; and ZP identifies the zero point contribution. The supplied adsorption free energies already include these corrections. We evaluated the four reduction potentials for the associative four electron ORR pathway with the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) method [10.1021/jp047349j]:
P00033  			[[MATH: U_{1} = (4.92 eV - ΔG_{OOH}) / e
U_{2} = (ΔG_{OOH} - ΔG_{O}) / e
U_{3} = (ΔG_{O} - ΔG_{OH}) / e
U_{4} = ΔG_{OH} / e]]	(5)
P00034  Equation (5) lists the positive step reduction potentials for four reductions: O₂ to OOH, OOH to O, O to OH, and OH to H₂O; e is the elementary charge. The limiting potential is the lowest of the four step potentials:
P00035  			[[MATH: U_{L} = min(U_{1}, U_{2}, U_{3}, U_{4})]]	(6)
P00036  The symbol on the left of Eq. (6) is the limiting potential. We evaluated the ORR overpotential relative to the 1.23 V equilibrium potential:
P00037  			[[MATH: η_{ORR} = 1.23 V - U_{L}]]	(7)
P00038  Equation (7) gives the ORR overpotential. Chemical subscripts in Eqs. (5)–(7) identify the adsorbed intermediate or the reaction quantity.
P00039  2.4. Scaling and thermodynamic activity analyses
P00040  We described the correlations of O and OOH adsorption free energies with OH adsorption free energy by ordinary least squares:
P00041  			[[MATH: ΔG_{O} = α_{O} ΔG_{OH} + β_{O}
ΔG_{OOH} = α_{OOH} ΔG_{OH} + β_{OOH}]]	(8)
P00042  In Eq. (8), α and β are the slope and intercept for the intermediate identified by the subscript. The curves in Figures 2 and 3 propagate the corresponding global fits through the four reaction steps. For comparison, we defined an ideal relation with an OOH–OH free energy separation of 2.46 eV and the corresponding two branch overpotential:
P00043  			[[MATH: ΔG_{OOH}^{ideal} = ΔG_{OH} + 2.46 eV
η_{ideal} = 1.23 V - min(ΔG_{OH} / e, (2.46 eV - ΔG_{OH}) / e)]]	(9)
P00044  In Eq. (9), OH removal defines the left branch and OOH formation the right branch. They meet at an OH adsorption free energy of 1.23 eV and zero overpotential. This reference represents balanced binding; the catalyst points and fitted curves use all four reaction steps. Thermodynamic rankings describe reaction free energies, while kinetic activity also depends on activation barriers.
P00045  2.5. Regression analysis and wider screening
P00046  The wider screening uses the DFT results for the 88 molecular catalysts as training data for linear regression models with second-order polynomial features. The adsorbate-specific models predict OH, O, and OOH adsorption free energies from catalyst descriptors and preserve parent-catalyst grouping during validation. We then apply the trained models to a separate ASE database containing exactly 1176 optimized clean catalysts. Catalyst-name comparison identifies 87 structures shared with the DFT library and 1089 structures outside it. The clean structures in the screening database were calculated with GPAW 25.7 and ASE 3.28.0 using the remaining settings stated in Section 2.2. The wider predictions are pending and are not reported as completed results.
P00047  Before the wider screening, we reproduced the supplied saved ridge models for OH, O, and OOH adsorption free energies. These models use eight predictors for OH and O and six for OOH. Figure 3 presents their descriptive predictions for the molecular structures represented in the current analysis. Because the plotted combinations include values used for model fitting, Figure 3 does not measure independent predictive accuracy or establish transfer to the 1089 structures outside the DFT library.
P00048  2.6. LLM manuscript generation
P00049  We used an LLM accessed through OpenAI Codex to develop and generate the complete manuscript from the scientific inputs. Ifargan and colleagues demonstrated data-to-paper generation with interacting LLM agents and source-linked outputs [10.1056/aioa2400555]. We focus on a complementary question: can extensive human guidance be consolidated into one final prompt that reproducibly generates a complete scientific article?
P00050  Development proceeded through repeated human-guided iterations. We supplied the scientific questions, databases, analyses, figures, literature, journal requirements, terminology, and writing guidance. Ten defined functions covered project curation, data auditing, programming, figure preparation, scientific supervision, drafting, domain review, document construction, style editing, and release auditing. We corrected scientific, terminological, structural, and stylistic failures during development and incorporated the resulting rules into the project knowledge bases.
P00051  The final execution will combine the verified scientific inputs and tested instructions in one prompt. We define success as generation of the complete article without subsequent textual correction, followed by human verification of the calculations, predictions, figures, citations, interpretations, and claims. This criterion separates reproducible execution from the substantial human effort required to develop the prompt. The human authors retain responsibility for the scientific questions, methods, interpretation, verification, and final approval.
P00052  3. Results and discussion
P00053  3.1. Molecular structures and adsorption centers
P00054  Figure 1 shows the common molecular structure and its variable components. Each DASC has one dual-atom site containing two metal-atom adsorption centers. Both metal positions draw from Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu; the bridge atoms and diamine vary, while the dialdehyde remains A2. An ORR intermediate can bind at either metal-atom center. We preserve that choice in the databases and in every comparison of adsorption energies.

P00056  Figure 1. Modular molecular structure of the catalyst library. The metal list gives the elements available at the two metal positions; bridge atoms are N, O, or S. A2, B1, and B2 follow the dialdehyde and diamine notation introduced in our data article [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. The arrows identify the metal region, bridge, diamine, and lower aromatic ring assigned to A2.
P00057  The final DFT library contains 88 molecular catalyst structures. Exact matching of the adsorption databases by catalyst and adsorption center produced 123 complete pathways across 82 catalysts. Figure 2 contains 118 filtered pathways across 79 catalysts. Figure 3 contains descriptive predictions for 144 catalyst and adsorption center combinations across 87 catalysts.
P00058  3.2. Adsorption scaling and calculated ORR thermodynamics
P00059  Figure 2a compares O and OOH adsorption free energies with OH adsorption free energy. The OOH–OH relation has a slope of 0.69 and an intercept of 2.98 eV; the O–OH relation has a slope of 1.66 and an intercept of 1.20 eV. Groups defined by the metal carrying the intermediate occupy different parts of these global relations. Related molecular catalysts show that paired metals can change OOH binding geometry [10.1021/jacs.4c12705; 10.1002/adma.202403549]. Our calculations permit comparisons across molecular structures without treating the two adsorption centers of one DASC as separate catalysts.
P00060  [embedded figure/image]
P00061  Figure 2. Adsorption scaling and oxygen reduction thermodynamics from DFT. Figure 1 defines the molecular components and the two metal-atom adsorption centers of each dual-atom site. (a) O and OOH adsorption free energies versus OH adsorption free energy. Color and marker shape identify the metal carrying the intermediate; the centered vertical stroke denotes O. Solid and dashed lines are OOH–OH and O–OH scaling fits. (b) CHE overpotential for the corresponding catalyst and adsorption center combinations. The solid curve follows the global scaling fits through all four reaction steps. The dotted ideal reference assumes an OOH–OH separation of 2.46 eV, with OH removal and OOH formation defining its left and right branches. The star marks zero overpotential at 1.23 eV. Lower overpotential appears higher on the reversed axis.
P00062  Figure 2b shows the calculated CHE overpotential, with the vertical axis reversed. The lowest value in the plotted dataset is 0.34 V for a Cr–Mn molecular catalyst with S bridge atoms and B1 diamine when the intermediates bind at its Mn adsorption center. The adsorption free energies are 0.89 eV for OH, 1.99 eV for O, and 3.41 eV for OOH. The corresponding step potentials are 1.51, 1.42, 1.10, and 0.89 V, so reduction of OH to water determines the limiting potential. This calculation prioritizes a molecular structure for further investigation; assigning a causal role to its bridge or diamine requires comparisons across matched structures.
P00063  3.3. Regression predictions and wider screening
P00064  We first used the supplied ridge models to test whether the stored catalyst descriptors preserve the broad adsorption trends within the represented molecular library. Figure 3 contains predictions for 144 catalyst and adsorption center combinations across 87 catalysts. Its OOH–OH fit has a slope of 0.67 and an intercept of 2.98 eV, and its O–OH fit has a slope of 1.73 and an intercept of 1.14 eV. These descriptive predictions follow the broad relations from the DFT calculations in Figure 2.
P00065  The lowest predicted overpotential in the current descriptive analysis is 0.30 V for an Fe–Mn molecular catalyst with S bridge atoms and B1 diamine when the intermediates bind at its Fe adsorption center. We would examine this molecular structure with further DFT calculations or experiment. The larger screening will train the final regression model on the 88-catalyst DFT library and apply it to all 1176 clean catalyst structures. Those predictions are not yet available, so Figure 3 does not identify candidates from the 1089 structures outside the DFT library.

P00068  3.4. Reusable data and further discovery
P00069  Reusable adsorption center matched data let us change the scientific question without rebuilding the dataset. The conventional CHE overpotential, the Calle-Vallejo electrochemical step symmetry index, and the Exner free energy span emphasize different features of the same reaction energetics [10.1016/j.coelec.2018.03.025; 10.1021/acscatal.2c03997]. When each energy remains attached to its molecular structure and adsorption center, another researcher can evaluate a different activity measure directly. Questions that change geometry, charge state, or adsorption structure still require new DFT calculations.
P00070  We will release the optimized structures and DFT calculations as ASE databases and machine readable JSON, together with code that reconstructs the analysis. These formats support findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable data practices [10.1038/sdata.2016.18]. Other researchers can combine these calculations with additional data, test analyses that we did not anticipate, and determine where another descriptor changes the molecular ranking.
P00071  3.5. Closing the scientific communication gap
P00072  The catalyst study illustrates why scientific writing belongs inside the discussion of discovery speed. DFT calculations create reusable scientific evidence, and ML extends its reach across a larger molecular library. Neither contribution can guide another research group until the methods, results, limitations, and data become accessible. The same separation applies beyond this catalyst family: researchers generate and verify scientific results, while a developed LLM system can compose the article that communicates them.
P00073  We developed the writing instructions through repeated interaction over approximately two weeks. The development stage required scientific corrections, terminology decisions, literature selection, document construction, and independent audits. The final execution condenses that work into one prompt. We will report its elapsed generation time as a project measurement, without treating our previous writing experience or journal publication delays as a controlled comparison.
P00074  This division of labor does not transfer scientific responsibility to the LLM. We choose the research questions, produce the DFT calculations, define and verify the ML predictions, interpret the results, and decide which statements the evidence permits. The LLM generates the scientific text. Human verification closes the chain before publication, and release of the data and final prompt makes both the catalyst analysis and manuscript generation open to examination.
P00075  4. Conclusions
P00076  The molecular DASC study establishes the scientific basis of the demonstration. DFT calculations for 88 catalysts revealed O–OH and OOH–OH adsorption free energy scaling and prioritized a Cr–Mn molecular structure with a calculated ORR overpotential of 0.34 V. The current regression analysis preserved the broad adsorption trends and prioritized an Fe–Mn molecular structure for further study. The final model will use the 88-catalyst DFT library to screen 1176 optimized catalysts, including 1089 structures outside that library. This extension can reduce the number of structures that require preliminary DFT calculations, although the wider predictions and their validation remain pending.
P00077  The main result we seek concerns how verified research becomes reusable knowledge. We developed a human-guided LLM writing system whose final execution will generate the complete article from one prompt. The article itself provides the test: its scientific statements must remain traceable to the calculations, predictions, figures, and literature supplied to the LLM. If the final execution requires no textual correction after human scientific verification, it will show that manuscript preparation can be delegated without delegating scientific responsibility. This reduces one communication delay while leaving peer review and publication outside the scope of the method. Researchers remain responsible for the questions, evidence, interpretation, verification, and final approval.
