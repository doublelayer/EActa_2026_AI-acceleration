P00001  Accelerating Catalyst Discovery and Design with Artificial Intelligence: Molecular Dual-Atom Site Catalysts as a Case Study
P00002  Bruno Inácio da Silva Roux Leite,1 Denis Savchenko,1 Timmo-Henrik Pukk,1 Ba Long Nguyen,1 Vladislav Ivanistsev,2† Nadezda Kongi,1†
P00003  1 Institute of Chemistry, University of Tartu, Ravila 14a, 50411 Tartu, Estonia
P00004  2 Department of Chemistry, University of Latvia, Jelgavas iela 1, Riga, LV-1004, Latvia

P00006  Abstract
P00007  Accelerating catalyst discovery requires timely analysis and clear communication as well as new calculations. We examine how a guided language model can help with these tasks through a study of oxygen reduction on molecular dual-atom site catalysts. We connect optimized molecular structures to adsorption scaling, thermodynamic activity estimates, and regression predictions. The two analyses recover the broad adsorption trends but prioritize different sites, which gives us a concrete reason to inspect the predictions and plan further calculations. We also use the language model to generate the complete article from the research record. This deliberate demonstration asks how much narrative labor researchers need to retain when they can guide and verify its production. We argue that the opportunity is to devote more attention to research design, implementation, and interpretation while delegating much of the analysis and reporting to artificial intelligence.
P00008  Keywords: artificial intelligence; computational hydrogen electrode; density functional theory; machine learning; oxygen reduction reaction; molecular dual-atom site catalysts
P00009  1. Introduction
P00010  Accelerating catalyst discovery with artificial intelligence (AI) requires us to ask where researchers actually spend their effort. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations provide adsorption energies, and regression models help us explore related structures [10.1002/adma.74833; 10.1002/adma.202403549; 10.1021/acscatal.6c02383]. Between those calculations and the next research decision, we must organize the data, choose an analysis, examine its meaning, and explain what we have learned. We argue that guided AI can shorten this part of the work as well. Our example is an article in which we direct a language model to analyze molecular catalyst data and write the account you are reading.
P00011  A research paper is expected to tell a convincing scientific story. The Nature Physics editorial Elements of style makes narrative the organizing principle of a paper, while the ACS Energy Letters editors connect a coherent theme with an effective discussion [10.1021/acsenergylett.8b02322; 10.1038/nphys724]. Related editorials ask us to guide readers through the purpose and interpretation of the work and to select figures that carry its central findings [10.1021/acsenergylett.1c02401; 10.1038/s41551-018-0202-5]. We accept that demand for clarity. We question whether composing the narrative must remain a major part of a researcher’s labor. If we can direct a language model to assemble a clear account from verifiable records, what should we reserve our attention for?
P00012  The answer depends on our control over the evidence. A polished story can conceal inconvenient observations, a danger discussed explicitly in Nature Methods [10.1038/nmeth.2726]. A language model can make such a story easier to produce, including claims or images that have no basis in the research. We therefore take responsibility for the questions, calculations, analytical choices, and interpretation. We give the model the records needed to express and inspect that work. This division of labor is the proposition we explore here.
P00013  We use the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) to make the proposition concrete. Its four electron pathway depends on the binding of O, OH, and OOH, so correlated adsorption free energies constrain the thermodynamic activity [10.1021/jp047349j]. Molecular dual-atom site catalysts give us a defined set in which metal identity, bridge, diamine, and adsorption site can be varied, see Figure 1. Related molecular catalysts show how binding geometry and the second metal can change oxygen intermediate energetics [10.1021/jacs.4c12705; 10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. We first follow those changes through the DFT data in Figure 2, then ask what a regression model retains in Figure 3. The same records also let us revisit the activity descriptor without repeating the underlying calculations. We use that progression to demonstrate how researcher guidance can turn AI assisted analysis and writing into a traceable research account.
P00014  2. Computational methods
P00015  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00016  We assembled the molecular catalysts from the components shown in Figure 1: two metal centers chosen from Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu; the A2 dialdehyde; a B1 or B2 diamine; and N, O, or S bridge atoms. The original database describes the molecular assembly and initial structure preparation [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. We fully optimized all structures used in the DFT analysis, including the clean catalysts and structures containing adsorbed intermediates. The clean database used here contains 88 molecular catalysts.
P00017  We retained the catalyst identity, metal positions, ligand components, and charge metadata in the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database.
P00018  2.2. Density functional theory calculations
P00019  We performed spin polarized DFT calculations using GPAW 26.7 and ASE 3.29.0 software [10.1063/5.0182685; 10.1088/1361-648X/aa680e]. We used the projector augmented wave method, the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and the D4 dispersion correction [10.1063/1.5090222]. The calculations used a plane wave cutoff of 550 eV, a real space grid spacing of 0.16 Å, 6.00 Å vacuum, and Γ point sampling for isolated molecules. We fully relaxed the atomic positions and converged the electronic density. We calculated gas phase H₂ and H₂O with the same settings. This methodology follows our work on related molecular catalysts [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1].
P00020  2.3. Adsorption structures and database matching
P00021  We placed O, OH, and OOH at the metal adsorption sites identified in Figure 1 and fully optimized each structure. For OOH, we retained the lower energy of the two tested initial orientations at each site. We joined all intermediate energies by catalyst name and adsorption site label. Thus, each reaction pathway refers to one specific metal atom in one molecular catalyst.
P00022  We defined adsorption energies from the total energies of the clean catalyst, adsorbed structures, and gas phase references using Eqs. (1)–(3). An asterisk denotes the clean adsorption site or a bound intermediate, as appropriate. The analysis used the supplied corrected adsorption free energies directly for scaling, filtering, and thermodynamic activity estimates.

P00033  2.5. Scaling and thermodynamic activity analyses
P00034  We fitted O–OH and OOH–OH adsorption free energy scaling relations by ordinary least squares using Eq. (8). We retained sites with OH adsorption free energy of at least 0.00 eV and O adsorption free energy no greater than 4.92 eV. The curves in Figures 2 and 3 propagate the corresponding global scaling fits through all four CHE steps. The electrochemical step symmetry index and free energy span offer complementary thermodynamic interpretations of the same adsorption records [10.1016/j.coelec.2018.03.025; 10.1021/acscatal.2c03997].
P00035  The dotted ideal reference in Eq. (9) assumes an OOH–OH free energy separation of 2.46 eV. OH removal defines its left branch and OOH formation its right branch. The branches meet at OH adsorption free energy 1.23 eV and zero overpotential. This reference illustrates balanced binding; the catalyst points and fitted volcano curves use all four reaction steps. Thermodynamic rankings describe reaction free energies, while kinetic activity also depends on activation barriers.

P00038  2.6. Regression analysis
P00039  We used fitted ridge regression models for OH, O, and OOH adsorption free energies using descriptors of the clean catalysts in Figure 1. These describe Bader charge and electron population, local spin, and ligand charge distribution. The supplied models contain eight predictors for OH and O and six for OOH. We applied the saved training medians, means, standard deviations, and coefficients to all available catalyst and adsorption site records. We then joined the three predictions at each site, applied the energy filters, and evaluated the overpotential with Eqs. (5)–(7).
P00040  Figure 3 includes every available site that passes these filters, including sites used for model fitting. It describes predicted trends across the library. We used the supplied validation predictions to check reproduction of the saved models; the all-site figure itself does not measure independent predictive accuracy.
P00041  2.7. AI assisted manuscript generation
P00042  We used a large language model (LLM), accessed through OpenAI Codex, to generate the complete manuscript and develop analysis and figure code under our direction. Our motivation was to examine a practical response to the growing use of AI in scientific writing. Analysis of biomedical abstracts documents substantial uptake of LLM assisted writing [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813]. That observation establishes the scale of adoption; it does not establish the reliability of any individual article. We address the risk that rapid generation can also multiply unsupported claims and invented figures by grounding this article in supplied calculations, explicit analysis choices, and identifiable sources.
P00043  We supplied the research question, databases, regression coefficients, literature, and writing instructions, then refined the analysis and text through successive corrections. We required figures to be generated by executable code from the molecular structures and numerical records. We used the LLM to organize the evidence, examine alternative analyses, and compose the narrative; we decided which questions to pursue and checked the resulting claims. The manuscript itself is part of this demonstration. Its coherence is useful only insofar as a reader can follow the account back to the calculations and assess our interpretation.
P00044  3. Results and discussion
P00045  3.1. Molecular design space and site identity
P00046  Figure 1 gives us a common molecular structure in which to compare the effects of changing individual components. Both metal positions draw from the same six elements, while the bridge and diamine provide further variation and the dialdehyde remains fixed. We define an adsorption site as the metal atom within the organic ligands to which an ORR intermediate binds. Keeping that atom identifiable lets us distinguish the two possible pathways in the same molecule.

P00049  The clean database contains 88 catalyst structures. Exact matching of the raw adsorption databases produced 123 complete pathways across 82 catalysts. The energy filters retained 118 pathways across 79 catalysts for Figure 2. Figure 3 extends the analysis to sites with available clean catalyst descriptors.
P00050  3.2. Adsorption scaling and calculated ORR thermodynamics
P00051  Figure 2a compares O and OOH adsorption free energies with OH adsorption free energy. The OOH–OH relation has a slope of 0.69 and an intercept of 2.98 eV; the O–OH relation has a slope of 1.66 and an intercept of 1.20 eV. The adsorption site families occupy different parts of these trends, with scatter around the global relations. Related molecular catalysts can alter OOH binding geometry and adsorption scaling through their paired metal centers [10.1021/jacs.4c12705; 10.1002/adma.202403549]. The present trends provide a basis for examining such structural effects within a common molecular structure.

P00054  Figure 2b shows the calculated conventional computational hydrogen electrode overpotential, with the vertical axis reversed. The lowest value in the filtered working dataset is 0.34 V at the Mn adsorption site of a Cr–Mn catalyst with an S–S bridge and B1 diamine. Its stored adsorption quantities are 0.89 eV for OH, 1.99 eV for O, and 3.41 eV for OOH; the OH to water step determines the limiting potential. This thermodynamic ranking applies to the present structures, corrections, matching rules, and filters. The curve propagates the global scaling fits through the four step reaction model, as described in the Figure 2 caption.
P00055  3.3. Scaling and activity from regression predictions
P00056  We next ask how much of this pattern the regression recovers. Figure 3 applies the same analysis to predicted adsorption free energies. Complete predictions were available for 148 sites across 88 catalysts, and the filters retained 144 sites across 87 catalysts. The predicted OOH–OH slope and intercept are 0.67 and 2.98 eV, while those for O–OH are 1.73 and 1.14 eV. Their proximity to the DFT relations in Figure 2 shows that the descriptors retain the broad variation across the molecular library. The narrower spread of the predictions also prompts a closer look at the individual rankings.
P00057  Near the volcano maximum, the distinction becomes consequential. The lowest predicted overpotential is 0.30 V at the Fe site of an Fe–Mn catalyst with S bridge atoms and B1 diamine. The DFT analysis in Figure 2 instead prioritizes a Mn site. We can use this disagreement to select a useful next calculation: inspect the binding structures and descriptors of these candidates, then test whether the predicted order survives. Figure 3 therefore helps us direct further research while displaying the broad trends that the regression has learned. Its all-site predictions include fitted records, so the figure describes the represented library rather than establishing transfer to new chemical families.

P00060  3.4. From reusable data to the next research question
P00061  The comparison between Figures 2 and 3 raises a further question: how much does candidate selection depend on the activity measure we choose? We can examine it with the same site matched records. The Calle-Vallejo electrochemical step symmetry index and the Exner free energy span emphasize different features of the reaction energetics [10.1016/j.coelec.2018.03.025; 10.1021/acscatal.2c03997]. Once the energies are stored with their molecular and site identities, we can ask an LLM to implement either analysis, inspect the code, and compare the resulting rankings. We reserve new DFT calculations for questions that change the geometry, charge state, or adsorption structure.
P00062  We will release the structures and results as an ASE database and machine readable JSON, together with code that reconstructs the analysis. These formats support findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable data practices [10.1038/sdata.2016.18]. They also make our interpretation open to revision: another researcher can choose a different descriptor, regenerate the figures, and identify where the conclusions change. The value of the record grows when it can answer a question that we did not originally ask.
P00063  We developed the instructions over about two weeks and estimate that executing the resulting instructions to generate an integrated draft takes less than an hour. In our experience, composing comparable articles manually has taken months. This account describes our project rather than a controlled timing study. The concrete result is that we can revise an analysis and its written explanation together, while directing our attention to the assumptions and decisions that determine what the result means.
P00064  4. Conclusions
P00065  Accelerating catalyst discovery with artificial intelligence includes accelerating how we analyze and report the research. We have used molecular catalyst calculations to make that possibility concrete: the same structured records support adsorption scaling, thermodynamic rankings, regression predictions, and alternative activity descriptors. A guided language model can help execute these analyses and explain their relationship. Where the rankings disagree, we gain a question for further research rather than a reason to force the results into one story.
P00066  We began with the demand for a convincing scientific story and delegated its composition to a language model. The resulting account still depends on the decisions we made about the molecules, calculations, evidence, and interpretation. We therefore propose a different division of effort: researchers should concentrate on planning and implementing the research, directing the analysis, and judging its conclusions, while guided models take on much of the narrative drafting. Clear communication remains essential. Its automation gives us a reason to invest more attention in the science that makes the story worth telling.
