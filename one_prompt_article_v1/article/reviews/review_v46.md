P00001  Accelerating Catalyst Discovery with Artificial Intelligence: Molecular Dual-Atom Site Catalysts as a Case Study
P00002  Bruno Inácio da Silva Roux Leite,1 Denis Savchenko,1 Timmo-Henrik Pukk,1 Ba Long Nguyen,1 Vladislav Ivanistsev,2† Nadezda Kongi,1†
P00003  1 Institute of Chemistry, University of Tartu, Ravila 14a, 50411 Tartu, Estonia

P00006  Abstract
P00007  Accelerating catalyst discovery with artificial intelligence (AI) requires accurate density functional theory (DFT) calculations, useful machine learning (ML) predictions, and clear scientific writing. We demonstrate this with the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) on molecular dual-atom site catalysts (DASCs). DFT calculations for 88 molecular catalysts identified three structures with calculated ORR overpotentials of 0.34, 0.39, and 0.41 V. Ridge regression trained on these results predicted adsorption free energies for 1176 optimized molecular catalysts. In the lowest predicted overpotential decile, Fe adsorption centers predominated; S bridge atoms and p-phenylenediamine were enriched relative to the full predicted set. A human guided large language model (LLM) prepared the complete article from verified calculations, ML predictions, three databases, literature, and figures. We directed the analysis and checked the article against the DFT calculations, ML predictions, and databases. By doing so we show how AI can accelerate catalyst discovery through ML predictions and LLM assisted scientific writing.
P00008  Keywords: AI; DFT; ML; LLM; oxygen reduction reaction; molecular dual-atom site catalysts
P00009  1. Introduction
P00010  Accelerating catalyst discovery with artificial intelligence requires reliable DFT calculations, useful ML predictions, and clear scientific writing. DFT calculations establish adsorption energetics, ML predictions extend the analysis to additional molecular structures, and databases preserve data for reuse. Scientific writing connects verified results to other researchers. Here, we demonstrate that a guided LLM can prepare a complete article from DFT calculations, ML predictions, databases, published literature, and explicit instructions.
P00011  Independent analyses place chemistry publication timelines at several months after submission. One journal sample reported an average interval of approximately nine months, while a study of chemistry articles published by the Public Library of Science reported a median submission to publication interval of 154 days [10.1016/j.joi.2013.09.001; 10.1007/s11192-023-04640-6]. These measurements begin at submission and exclude preparation of the first manuscript and revisions after rejection. Manuscript preparation therefore adds time before the publication interval begins.
P00012  Editors have argued that a coherent scientific story and carefully selected figures help readers understand a paper [10.1038/nphys724; 10.1021/acsenergylett.8b02322; 10.1021/acsenergylett.1c02401; 10.1038/s41551-018-0202-5]. Clear writing supports research, but writing skill alone does not establish scientific value. Our thesis is direct: researchers can delegate article writing to a guided LLM when DFT calculations, ML predictions, and databases constrain every claim. The story must follow the data.
P00013  Catalyst databases can support analyses that their creators did not anticipate. Researchers can combine datasets, re-evaluate data points, and test new interpretations [10.1038/sdata.2016.18]. A database preserves molecular structures and calculated values; each article presents specific analyses of those data. Reusable databases let researchers test further questions against the same scientific results.
P00014  Vocabulary analyses of published abstracts have documented changes associated with LLM assisted writing [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813]. Editorials also warn that story driven selection can hide conflicting observations [10.1038/nmeth.2726]. Ifargan and colleagues showed that interacting LLM agents can prepare human verifiable papers from annotated data [10.1056/aioa2400555]. We build on this work by supplying the LLM with verified DFT calculations, ML predictions, three databases, literature, figures, and project instructions.
P00015  Our study links three stages. DFT calculations establish adsorption energetics for 88 molecular catalysts. ML predictions extend the analysis to 1176 optimized molecular structures and identify catalyst features associated with lower predicted overpotentials. A guided LLM then prepares the complete article from the verified results. Figure 1 shows the molecular variables, Figure 2 presents DFT scaling relations and ORR thermodynamics, and Figure 3 presents the corresponding ML predictions. Together, these results show how artificial intelligence can accelerate catalyst discovery through DFT calculations, ML predictions, and scientific writing.
P00016  2. Computational methods
P00017  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00018  The molecular catalysts were assembled from the components shown in Figure 1. Our previous data article defined B1 as the 1,2-phenylenediamine fragment and B2 as a 1,8-naphthalenediamine fragment [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. A2 is the fixed dialdehyde – 2-hydroxyisophthalaldehyde. Each molecule contains one dual-atom site with two metal-atom adsorption centers. The two metal positions were occupied by Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, or Cu, and each of the two bridge positions contained N, O, or S. Every clean and adsorbate structure in the DFT analysis was fully optimized. Three linked molecular catalyst databases were used: the published structure database, the fixed DFT dataset of 88 catalysts, and the larger dataset of 1176 optimized catalysts used for ML predictions. Each optimized structure and its energy were stored in an Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database with the catalyst name, metal identities, ligand components, and charge metadata. The `ads_site` field identifies the metal-atom adsorption center occupied by each intermediate.
P00019  2.2. DFT calculations and adsorption structures
P00020  Spin polarized DFT calculations were performed with GPAW 25.7 and ASE 3.28.0 software [10.1063/5.0182685; 10.1088/1361-648X/aa680e]. The projector augmented wave method, revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and D4 dispersion correction were used [10.1063/1.5090222]. A plane wave cutoff of 550 eV, real space grid spacing of 0.16 Å, and 6.00 Å of vacuum were applied. Each atomic structure was optimized until the maximum force fell below 0.05 eV Å⁻¹, and only calculations that converged with these parameters entered the fixed DFT set. The setup follows earlier work on related molecular catalysts [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. The total charge was set to +2 for every modeled catalyst, including N bridged structures. This consistent cohort represents one charge assignment and the spin states reached by the spin polarized optimizations. A broader electronic structure survey should compare alternative charges, mixed oxidation states, and spin states.
P00021
P00022  O, OH, and OOH were placed at either metal-atom adsorption center in the dual-atom site, and each structure was fully optimized. Gas phase H₂ and H₂O reference structures were calculated with the same settings. Total energies were matched by catalyst name and `ads_site`, so each reaction pathway contains values for the same molecular structure and adsorption center.
P00023  Adsorption energies for O, OH, and OOH were calculated from the total energies of the corresponding adsorbed structure, clean catalyst, H₂, and H₂O:
P00024  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OH} = E_{*OH} + (1/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(1)
P00025  			[[MATH: ΔE_{O} = E_{*O} + E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(2)
P00026  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OOH} = E_{*OOH} + (3/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - 2E_{H_{2}O}]]	(3)
P00027  In Eqs. (1)–(3), the left side is the adsorption energy of the named intermediate. The terms on the right are the total energies of the adsorbed catalyst, clean catalyst, gas phase H₂, and gas phase H₂O; * identifies the selected adsorption center. The supplied adsorption free energies were used in the scaling and thermodynamic analyses.
P00028  2.3. Adsorption free energies and ORR thermodynamics
P00029  Adsorption energies were converted to adsorption free energies by adding zero point and entropic corrections:
P00030  			[[MATH: ΔG_{X} = ΔE_{X} + ΔE_{ZP,X} - TΔS_{X}]]	(4)
P00031  In Eq. (4), X denotes O, OH, or OOH; E and G denote electronic energy and Gibbs free energy; T is temperature; S is entropy; and ZP identifies the zero point contribution. The supplied adsorption free energies already include these corrections. The four reduction potentials for the associative four electron ORR pathway were evaluated with the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) method [10.1021/jp047349j]:
P00032  			[[MATH: U_{1} = (4.92 eV - ΔG_{OOH}) / e
U_{2} = (ΔG_{OOH} - ΔG_{O}) / e
U_{3} = (ΔG_{O} - ΔG_{OH}) / e
U_{4} = ΔG_{OH} / e]]	(5)
P00033  Equation (5) defines the positive reduction potentials for the four steps O₂ to OOH, OOH to O, O to OH, and OH to H₂O; * identifies an adsorbed intermediate and e is the elementary charge. The limiting potential is defined as the lowest of the four step potentials:
P00034  			[[MATH: U_{L} = min(U_{1}, U_{2}, U_{3}, U_{4})]]	(6)
P00035  The symbol on the left of Eq. (6) denotes the limiting potential. The ORR overpotential was evaluated relative to the equilibrium potential of 1.23 V:
P00036  			[[MATH: η_{ORR} = 1.23 V - U_{L}]]	(7)
P00037  Equation (7) defines the ORR overpotential. Chemical subscripts in Eqs. (5)–(7) identify the adsorbed intermediate or reaction quantity.
P00038  2.4. Scaling and thermodynamic activity analyses
P00039  The relations of O and OOH adsorption free energies to OH adsorption free energy were evaluated by ordinary least squares:
P00040  			[[MATH: ΔG_{O} = α_{O} ΔG_{OH} + β_{O}
ΔG_{OOH} = α_{OOH} ΔG_{OH} + β_{OOH}]]	(8)
P00041  In Eq. (8), α and β are the slope and intercept for the intermediate identified by the subscript. The fitted relations were propagated through the four ORR steps to obtain the scaling based activity curve in Figures 2 and 3. An ideal reference relation was defined with an OOH–OH free energy separation of 2.46 eV:
P00042  			[[MATH: ΔG_{OOH}^{ideal} = ΔG_{OH} + 2.46 eV
η_{ideal} = 1.23 V - min(ΔG_{OH} / e, (2.46 eV - ΔG_{OH}) / e)]]	(9)
P00043  In Eq. (9), OH removal defines the left branch and OOH formation defines the right branch. The branches meet at an OH adsorption free energy of 1.23 eV and zero overpotential. Catalyst activity was interpreted thermodynamically; kinetic activity also depends on activation barriers.
P00044  2.5. Regression analysis and catalyst-family screening
P00045  The adsorption free energies from the fixed 88 catalyst DFT dataset were used to train separate ridge regression models for OH, O, and OOH. Descriptors of the clean catalysts represented the electron population, charge, spin, metal pair polarization, and ligand environment. Eight predictors were used for OH and O, and six were used for OOH. The parameter tables retain the training medians, means, standard deviations, standardized coefficients, and ridge parameters. The ridge parameter was 0.10 for OH and 1.00 for O and OOH. The models were applied to the separate database of 1176 optimized structures described in Section 2.1.
P00046  The predicted OH, O, and OOH values were joined by catalyst name and `ads_site` before the CHE step potentials and overpotentials were evaluated. This produced 2080 complete catalyst and adsorption center combinations across all 1176 structures. The Figure 2 energy filters retained 2021 combinations across 1156 catalysts for Figure 3. For comparison of structural families, the most active pool in each DFT or ML dataset was defined as the lowest 10% of retained overpotentials, rounded upward to a whole catalyst and adsorption center combination.
P00047  2.6. LLM manuscript generation
P00048  An LLM accessed through OpenAI Codex was used to prepare the complete manuscript from the calculations, model predictions, figures, literature, and project instructions. Data to paper generation with interacting LLM agents and source linked outputs has been reported by Ifargan and colleagues [10.1056/aioa2400555]. The present procedure was developed for one consolidated prompt that generates a complete electrochemistry article after extensive human guidance.
P00049  The manuscript instructions were developed through repeated human guidance over approximately two weeks. Scientific terminology, source authority, database matching, analysis code, figure construction, journal requirements, article structure, and writing style were tested and corrected. Ten defined functions covered project curation, scientific auditing, programming, figure preparation, supervision, drafting, literature synthesis, document construction, style editing, and release auditing.
P00050  The consolidated prompt generated the complete article from verified scientific inputs and tested instructions. Human researchers checked the calculations, ML predictions, figures, citations, terminology, and scientific interpretation before approving the article.

P00057  3.2. Adsorption scaling and calculated ORR thermodynamics
P00058  Figure 2a relates the DFT calculated O and OOH adsorption free energies to OH adsorption free energy for the 118 retained catalyst and adsorption center combinations. The OOH–OH fit has a slope of 0.69 and an intercept of 2.98 eV, while the O–OH fit has a slope of 1.66 and an intercept of 1.20 eV. The steeper O–OH relation gives O binding the larger response to changes in OH binding. Figure 2b carries both fits through the four CHE steps, so the adsorption relations and the calculated ORR overpotential remain part of one thermodynamic analysis.

All three contain S bridge atoms, while their metal pairs and diamines differ. These DFT calculations identify three molecular structures with the lowest calculated ORR overpotentials in the analyzed dataset.
P00062  3.3. Regression predictions across catalyst families
P00063  The lowest DFT overpotential decile contains 12 catalyst and adsorption center combinations. Five have Co adsorption centers, four have Mn, and three have Fe; none have Cr, Ni, or Cu adsorption centers. Seven contain S–S bridges, four contain N–N bridges, and one contains an O–O bridge. The p-phenylenediamine and naphthalenediamine each occur six times. The DFT pool therefore selects a restricted set of adsorption-center metals and moderately favors S bridge atoms, while it shows no preference between the diamines.
P00064  The ML predictions sharpen the metal trend across the larger database. The lowest predicted decile contains 203 catalyst and adsorption center combinations: 187 have Fe adsorption centers and 16 have Co adsorption centers. S–S bridges occur in 65 combinations, or 32%, compared with 18% in the full predicted set. The p-phenylenediamine occurs in 122 combinations, or 60%, compared with 54% in the full set. The strongest design principle is an Fe adsorption center in an S bridged molecular structure, with p-phenylenediamine as a secondary preference and Co as the next metal family. No single component determines activity by itself. The three lowest individual predictions illustrate this pattern: an Fe–Fe DASC with A9 dialdehyde, O bridge atoms, and p-phenylenediamine gives 0.29 V; an Fe–Fe DASC with A1 dialdehyde, S bridge atoms, and p-phenylenediamine gives 0.30 V; and an Mn–Fe DASC with A9 dialdehyde, S bridge atoms, and p-phenylenediamine at the Fe adsorption center gives 0.30 V. These predictions are conditional on the charge and spin treatment described in Section 2.2.

P00067  3.4. Molecular catalyst design in context
P00068  Our data article defines A2 as the dialdehyde, B1 as the p-phenylenediamine fragment, and B2 as the naphthalenediamine fragment [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. A related study of this molecular catalyst family found that phenylene and naphthalene spacers alter the distance between Co centers, oxygen intermediate binding geometry, and ORR selectivity [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. Other molecular studies report bidentate OOH binding and changes in metal coordination with bridge and ligand structure [10.1021/jacs.4c12705; 10.1021/acscatal.6c00046]. These studies support examining metal pair, bridge, diamine, and adsorption center as connected structural variables.
P00069  Zhang and colleagues provide the closest precedent through catalysis-unit engineering [10.1002/adma.202403549]. They evaluated 60 homonuclear salphen units assembled from ten metals and six coordination environments, calculated oxygen evolution reaction (OER) free energies and scaling relations with DFT, ranked 27 descriptors with XGBoost, and used the Sure Independence Screening and Sparsifying Operator (SISSO) to build an interpretable model before incorporating selected Ni and Co units into salphen covalent organic materials. Our ORR study covers 88 molecular catalysts in the DFT stage, includes homo- and heteronuclear metal pairs, resolves adsorption at either metal center, and extends prediction to 1176 optimized structures. The reactions and regression models differ, yet both studies show how DFT results can train ML models to identify structural trends across catalyst families.
P00070  That division is also visible in recent screening of extended phthalocyanine dual-atom catalysts, where 81 DFT structures supported predictions for 360 additional metal pairs and identified Fe as an adsorption anchor whose neighboring metal regulates activity [10.1002/adma.74833]. In our data, DFT establishes the favorable S bridged examples, and ML carries the metal and ligand comparison across an order of magnitude more molecular structures. The regression screen therefore substitutes fast predictions for many preliminary adsorption calculations; targeted DFT and experiment can then concentrate on the families selected from that screen. This is the specific way in which ML accelerates catalyst discovery in the present study.
P00071  3.5. Alternative activity descriptors and reusable DFT data
P00072  The same DFT calculations, matched by catalyst and adsorption center, support several thermodynamic analyses of ORR. The conventional CHE overpotential is set by the least favorable individual reduction step. Calle-Vallejo's electrochemical step symmetry index evaluates how evenly the four step potentials divide the 1.23 V reaction free energy, while Exner's free energy span identifies the largest cumulative uphill change across consecutive reaction states at a selected potential [10.1016/j.coelec.2018.03.025; 10.1021/acscatal.2c03997]. Each descriptor asks a different question of the same adsorption free energies. Because catalyst and adsorption center identities remain attached to every value, other researchers can apply these analyses directly to the same dataset.
P00073  The DFT dataset preserves calculated adsorption energies with the matching catalyst and adsorption center identities. The database structure allows researchers to apply other ORR descriptors and compare their results with the analyses reported here.
P00074  3.6. Closing the scientific communication gap
P00075  The catalyst study gives the LLM verified DFT calculations and ML predictions to communicate. The DFT calculations describe adsorption energetics for the 88 catalyst dataset, and ML predictions extend the analysis to 1176 optimized structures. The databases preserve molecular structures, adsorption center identities, and calculated or predicted values. The article presents these results with the methods, figures, and literature needed to interpret them.
P00076  Over approximately two weeks, we taught the LLM the project terminology, evidence hierarchy, scientific methods, journal rules, figure standards, and manuscript voice. We checked successive drafts against the ASE databases, analysis outputs, literature, and our scientific interpretation. Those corrections became reusable instructions, and the consolidated prompt generated the complete article. The development required sustained human judgment; the final execution gathered that judgment into a repeatable writing procedure.
P00077  We chose the research questions, produced and verified the DFT results, directed the ML analysis, interpreted the structural trends, and decided what the evidence supports. The LLM composed the article from those verified inputs. This division lets researchers concentrate on scientific method and discovery while a prepared LLM carries the writing. The same division raises the standard for source traceability, data organization, and human review because fluent prose must remain answerable to the science.
P00078  4. Conclusions
P00079  We demonstrate accelerating catalyst discovery with artificial intelligence by using a guided LLM to generate the complete article from verified DFT calculations, ML predictions, databases, literature, and figures. We directed the investigation and checked the article against its scientific inputs. This completed study shows that a developed LLM can prepare scientific writing while researchers direct the scientific work.
P00080  DFT calculations for 88 molecular catalysts identified three S bridged structures with low calculated ORR overpotentials. ML predictions extended the analysis to 1176 molecular catalysts and associated the lowest predicted overpotentials with Fe adsorption centers, S bridge atoms, and p-phenylenediamine. These predictions guide selection of structures for further study. Together, the DFT calculations, ML predictions, and LLM assisted scientific writing demonstrate how artificial intelligence can accelerate catalyst discovery.
