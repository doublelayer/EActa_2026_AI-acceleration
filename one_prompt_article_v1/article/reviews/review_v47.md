P00011  1. Introduction
P00012  Independent analyses place chemistry publication timelines at several months after submission. One journal sample reported an average interval of approximately nine months, while a study of chemistry articles published by the Public Library of Science reported a median submission to publication interval of 154 days [10.1016/j.joi.2013.09.001; 10.1007/s11192-023-04640-6]. These measurements begin at submission and exclude preparation of the first manuscript and revisions after rejection. Manuscript preparation therefore adds time before the publication interval even begins.
P00013  Editors have argued that a coherent scientific story and carefully selected figures help readers understand a paper [10.1038/nphys724; 10.1021/acsenergylett.8b02322; 10.1021/acsenergylett.1c02401; 10.1038/s41551-018-0202-5]. Clear writing supports research, but writing skill alone does not establish scientific value. Our thesis is direct: researchers can delegate article writing to a guided LLM when DFT calculations, ML predictions, and databases constrain every claim. The story must follow the data.
P00014  Catalyst databases can support analyses that their creators did not anticipate. Researchers can combine datasets, re-evaluate data points, and test new interpretations [10.1038/sdata.2016.18]. A database preserves molecular structures and calculated values; each article presents specific analyses of those data. Reusable databases let researchers test further questions against the same scientific results.
P00015  Vocabulary analyses of published abstracts have documented changes associated with LLM assisted writing [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813]. Editorials also warn that story driven selection can hide conflicting observations [10.1038/nmeth.2726]. Ifargan and colleagues showed that interacting LLM agents can prepare human verifiable papers from annotated data [10.1056/aioa2400555]. We build on this work by supplying the LLM with verified DFT calculations, ML predictions, three databases, literature, figures, and project instructions.
P00016  Our study links three stages. DFT calculations establish adsorption energetics for 88 molecular catalysts. ML predictions extend the analysis to 1176 optimized molecular structures and identify catalyst features associated with lower predicted overpotentials. A guided LLM then prepares the complete article from the verified results. Figure 1 shows the molecular variables, Figure 2 presents DFT scaling relations and ORR thermodynamics, and Figure 3 presents the corresponding ML predictions. Together, these results show how artificial intelligence can accelerate catalyst discovery through DFT calculations, ML predictions, and scientific writing.
P00017  2. Computational methods
P00018  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00019  The molecular catalysts were assembled from the components shown in Figure 1. Our previous data article defined B1 as the 1,2-phenylenediamine fragment and B2 as a 1,8-naphthalenediamine fragment [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. A2 is the fixed dialdehyde – 2-hydroxyisophthalaldehyde. Each molecule contains one dual-atom site with two metal-atom adsorption centers. The two metal positions were occupied by Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, or Cu, and each of the two bridge positions contained N, O, or S. Every clean and adsorbate structure in the DFT analysis was fully optimized. Three linked molecular catalyst databases were used: the published structure database, the fixed DFT dataset of 88 catalysts, and the larger dataset of 1176 optimized catalysts used for ML predictions. Each optimized structure and its energy were stored in an Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database with the catalyst name, metal identities, ligand components, and charge metadata. The ads_site field identifies the metal-atom adsorption center occupied by each intermediate.
P00020  2.2. DFT calculations and adsorption structures
P00021  Spin polarized DFT calculations were performed with GPAW 25.7 and ASE 3.28.0 software [10.1063/5.0182685; 10.1088/1361-648X/aa680e]. The projector augmented wave method, revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and D4 dispersion correction were used [10.1063/1.5090222]. A plane wave cutoff of 550 eV, real space grid spacing of 0.16 Å, and 6.00 Å of vacuum were applied. Each atomic structure was optimized until the maximum force fell below 0.05 eV Å⁻¹, and only calculations that converged with these parameters entered the fixed DFT set. The setup follows earlier work on related molecular catalysts [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. The total charge was set to +2 for every modeled catalyst, including N bridged structures. This consistent cohort represents one charge assignment and the spin states reached by the spin polarized optimizations. A broader electronic structure survey should compare alternative charges, mixed oxidation states, and spin states.

Gas phase H₂ and H₂O reference structures were calculated with the same settings. Total energies were matched by catalyst name and `ads_site`, so each reaction pathway contains values for the same molecular structure and adsorption center.

P00045  2.5. Regression analysis and catalyst-family screening
P00046  The adsorption free energies from the fixed 88 catalyst DFT dataset were used to train separate ridge regression models for OH, O, and OOH. Descriptors of the clean catalysts represented the electron population, charge, spin, metal pair polarization, and ligand environment. Eight predictors were used for OH and O, and six were used for OOH. The parameter tables retain the training medians, means, standard deviations, standardized coefficients, and ridge parameters. The ridge parameter was 0.10 for OH and 1.00 for O and OOH. The models were applied to the separate database of 1176 optimized structures described in Section 2.1.
P00047  The predicted OH, O, and OOH values were joined by catalyst name and `ads_site` before the CHE step potentials and overpotentials were evaluated. This produced 2080 complete catalyst and adsorption center combinations across all 1176 structures. The Figure 2 energy filters retained 2021 combinations across 1156 catalysts for Figure 3. For comparison of structural families, the most active pool in each DFT or ML dataset was defined as the lowest 10% of retained overpotentials, rounded upward to a whole catalyst and adsorption center combination.
P00048  2.6. LLM manuscript generation
P00049  An LLM accessed through OpenAI Codex was used to prepare the complete manuscript from the calculations, model predictions, figures, literature, and project instructions. Data to paper generation with interacting LLM agents and source linked outputs has been reported by Ifargan and colleagues [10.1056/aioa2400555]. The present procedure was developed for one consolidated prompt that generates a complete electrochemistry article after extensive human guidance.
P00050  The manuscript instructions were developed through repeated human guidance over approximately two weeks. Scientific terminology, source authority, database matching, analysis code, figure construction, journal requirements, article structure, and writing style were tested and corrected. Ten defined functions covered project curation, scientific auditing, programming, figure preparation, supervision, drafting, literature synthesis, document construction, style editing, and release auditing.
P00051  The consolidated prompt generated the complete article from verified scientific inputs and tested instructions. Human researchers checked the calculations, ML predictions, figures, citations, terminology, and scientific interpretation before approving the article.

P00063  3.3. Regression predictions across catalyst families
P00064  The lowest DFT overpotential decile contains 12 catalyst and adsorption center combinations. Five have Co adsorption centers, four have Mn, and three have Fe; none have Cr, Ni, or Cu adsorption centers. Seven contain S–S bridges, four contain N–N bridges, and one contains an O–O bridge. The 1,2-phenylenediamine and 1,8-naphthalenediamine each occur six times. The DFT pool therefore selects a restricted set of adsorption-center metals and moderately favors S bridge atoms, while it shows no preference between the diamines.
P00065  The ML predictions sharpen the metal trend across the larger database. The lowest predicted decile contains 203 catalyst and adsorption center combinations: 187 have Fe adsorption centers and 16 have Co adsorption centers. S–S bridges occur in 65 combinations, or 32%, compared with 18% in the full predicted set. The 1,2-phenylenediamine occurs in 122 combinations, or 60%, compared with 54% in the full set. The strongest design principle is an Fe adsorption center in an S bridged molecular structure, with 1,2-phenylenediamine as a secondary preference and Co as the next metal family. No single component determines activity by itself. The three lowest individual predictions illustrate this pattern: an Fe–Fe DASC with A9 dialdehyde, O bridge atoms, and 1,2-phenylenediamine gives 0.29 V; an Fe–Fe DASC with A1 dialdehyde, S bridge atoms, and 1,2-phenylenediamine gives 0.30 V; and an Mn–Fe DASC with A9 dialdehyde, S bridge atoms, and 1,2-phenylenediamine at the Fe adsorption center gives 0.30 V. These predictions are conditional on the charge and spin treatment described in Section 2.2.

P00068  3.4. Molecular catalyst design in context
P00069  Our data article defines A2 as the dialdehyde, B1 as the 1,2-phenylenediamine fragment, and B2 as the 1,8-naphthalenediamine fragment [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806]. A related study of this molecular catalyst family found that phenylene and naphthalene spacers alter the distance between Co centers, oxygen intermediate binding geometry, and ORR selectivity [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. Other molecular studies report bidentate OOH binding and changes in metal coordination with bridge and ligand structure [10.1021/jacs.4c12705; 10.1021/acscatal.6c00046]. These studies support examining metal pair, bridge, diamine, and adsorption center as connected structural variables.
P00070  Zhang and colleagues provide the closest precedent through catalysis-unit engineering [10.1002/adma.202403549]. They evaluated 60 homonuclear salphen units assembled from ten metals and six coordination environments, calculated oxygen evolution reaction (OER) free energies and scaling relations with DFT, ranked 27 descriptors with XGBoost, and used the Sure Independence Screening and Sparsifying Operator (SISSO) to build an interpretable model before incorporating selected Ni and Co units into salphen covalent organic materials. Our ORR study covers 88 molecular catalysts in the DFT stage, includes homo- and heteronuclear metal pairs, resolves adsorption at either metal center, and extends prediction to 1176 optimized structures. The reactions and regression models differ, yet both studies show how DFT results can train ML models to identify structural trends across catalyst families.
P00071  That division is also visible in recent screening of extended phthalocyanine dual-atom catalysts, where 81 DFT structures supported predictions for 360 additional metal pairs and identified Fe as an adsorption anchor whose neighboring metal regulates activity [10.1002/adma.74833]. In our data, DFT establishes the favorable S bridged examples, and ML carries the metal and ligand comparison across an order of magnitude more molecular structures. The regression screen therefore substitutes fast predictions for many preliminary adsorption calculations; targeted DFT and experiment can then concentrate on the families selected from that screen. This is the specific way in which ML accelerates catalyst discovery in the present study.
P00072  3.5. Alternative activity descriptors and reusable DFT data
P00073  The same DFT calculations, matched by catalyst and adsorption center, support several thermodynamic analyses of ORR. The conventional CHE overpotential is set by the least favorable individual reduction step. Calle-Vallejo's electrochemical step symmetry index evaluates how evenly the four step potentials divide the 1.23 V reaction free energy, while Exner's free energy span identifies the largest cumulative uphill change across consecutive reaction states at a selected potential [10.1016/j.coelec.2018.03.025; 10.1021/acscatal.2c03997]. Each descriptor asks a different question of the same adsorption free energies. Because catalyst and adsorption center identities remain attached to every value, other researchers can apply these analyses directly to the same dataset.
P00074  The DFT dataset preserves calculated adsorption energies with the matching catalyst and adsorption center identities. The database structure allows researchers to apply other ORR descriptors and compare their results with the analyses reported here.
