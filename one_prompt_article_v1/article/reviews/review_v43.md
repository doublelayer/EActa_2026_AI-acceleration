P00009  1. Introduction
P00010  Accelerating catalyst discovery has usually meant faster calculations, larger candidate sets, or better prioritization. Each can help researchers choose the next structure to calculate or test. Yet a result becomes useful to another group only after its evidence and reasoning are communicated. [[INSERT id=14; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:30:00Z: In this work, ]][[DELETE id=15; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:30:00Z: W]][[INSERT id=16; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:30:00Z: w]]e examine both stages through ORR on molecular DASCs, and we ask whether an LLM can carry the scientific writing that connects verified results to further research.
P00011  Independent analyses place chemistry publication timelines at several months after submission, with reported averages of approximately nine months across a journal sample and a median of 154 days for chemistry articles on the PLOS platform [10.1016/j.joi.2013.09.001; 10.1007/s11192-023-04640-6]. [[INSERT id=17; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:53:00Z: Morevever, ]][[DELETE id=18; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:53:00Z: T]][[INSERT id=19; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:53:00Z: t]]he time required to prepare a manuscript adds to this interval. [[INSERT id=20; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: Thus, ]][[DELETE id=21; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: S]][[INSERT id=22; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: s]]cientific writing [[DELETE id=23; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: therefore ]]belongs in any practical account of how quickly research can reach the wider community.
P00012  Editors have argued that a coherent story should organize a paper and that carefully chosen figures carry much of its message [10.1038/nphys724; 10.1021/acsenergylett.8b02322; 10.1021/acsenergylett.1c02401; 10.1038/s41551-018-0202-5]. We accept the value of a clear [[DELETE id=24; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: account]][[INSERT id=25; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: communciation]], yet we challenge the idea that researchers must spend months shaping every [[DELETE id=26; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: article ]][[INSERT id=27; author=Vladislav Ivaništšev; date=2026-10-12T20:54:00Z: storytelling ]]themselves. The story should explain the evidence; the evidence should determine the story.

P00014  LLMs can help close this communication gap, provided that their prose remains answerable to evidence. Scientific stories can conceal inconvenient observations when coherence becomes the goal [10.1038/nmeth.2726], while changes in published abstract vocabulary show that LLM-assisted writing has entered routine use [10.1126/sciadv.adt3813]. Ifargan and colleagues generated human-verifiable papers from annotated research data with interacting LLM agents [10.1056/aioa2400555]. We extend this line of work by testing whether a carefully developed set of scientific and editorial instructions can generate a complete article from verified research inputs.
P00015  Our study links three stages. We first calculated ORR adsorption energetics for 88 molecular DASCs with DFT. We then used regression predictions to identify patterns across the available molecular set and to prioritize structural families for further investigation. Finally, we used a human-guided LLM procedure to prepare the complete scientific article from the calculations, predictions, literature, figures, and writing instructions. Figure 1 defines the molecular variation; Figure 2 presents the DFT scaling relations and CHE volcano; Figure 3 presents the regression-predicted relations and activity. The article itself is the demonstration: the LLM prepares the text, while human researchers direct and verify the science.
P00016  2. Computational methods
P00017  2.1. Catalyst structures
P00018  The molecular catalysts were assembled from the components shown in Figure 1. The notation in the data article [10.1016/j.dib.2026.112806] identifies A2 as the fixed dialdehyde and B1 and B2 as the two diamines. Each molecule contains a dual-atom site with two metal-atom adsorption centers. The metal identities were selected from Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu, and the bridge atoms were selected from N, O, or S. All catalyst and adsorbate structures used in the DFT analysis were fully optimized. The clean database contained 88 molecular catalysts.
P00019  Each optimized structure and its energy were stored in an Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) database with the catalyst name, metal identities, ligand components, and charge metadata. The `ads_site` field identifies the metal-atom adsorption center occupied by each intermediate.
P00020  2.2. DFT calculations and adsorption structures
P00021  Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed with GPAW 25.7 and ASE 3.28.0 software [10.1063/5.0182685; 10.1088/1361-648X/aa680e]. The projector augmented wave method, the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional, and the D4 dispersion correction were used [10.1063/1.5090222]. A plane-wave cutoff of 550 eV, a real-space grid spacing of 0.16 Å, and 6.00 Å of vacuum were used. Every atomic structure was optimized until the maximum force fell below 0.05 eV Å⁻¹. The same calculation setup was previously used for related molecular catalysts [10.26434/chemrxiv.15001712/v1]. The total charge was set to +2 for every modeled catalyst, including N-bridged structures. Alternative total charges and systematic comparisons among spin states were outside the present calculation set. Accordingly, interpretation of the predicted activity rankings is restricted to this charge assignment and the spin states represented in the spin-polarized calculations.
P00022
P00023  O, OH, and OOH were placed at either metal-atom adsorption center in the dual-atom site, and each structure was fully optimized. Gas phase H₂ and H₂O reference structures were calculated with the same settings. Total energies were matched by catalyst name and `ads_site`, so each reaction pathway contains values for the same molecular structure and adsorption center.
P00024  Adsorption energies for O, OH, and OOH were calculated from the total energies of the corresponding adsorbed structure, clean catalyst, H₂, and H₂O:
P00025  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OH} = E_{*OH} + (1/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(1)
P00026  			[[MATH: ΔE_{O} = E_{*O} + E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - E_{H_{2}O}]]	(2)
P00027  			[[MATH: ΔE_{OOH} = E_{*OOH} + (3/2) E_{H_{2}} - E_{*} - 2E_{H_{2}O}]]	(3)
P00028  In Eqs. (1)–(3), the left side is the adsorption energy of the named intermediate. The terms on the right are the total energies of the adsorbed catalyst, clean catalyst, gas phase H₂, and gas phase H₂O; * identifies the selected adsorption center. The supplied adsorption free energies were used in the scaling and thermodynamic analyses.
P00029  2.3. Adsorption free energies and ORR thermodynamics
P00030  Adsorption energies were converted to adsorption free energies by adding zero point and entropic corrections:
P00031  			[[MATH: ΔG_{X} = ΔE_{X} + ΔE_{ZP,X} - TΔS_{X}]]	(4)
P00032  In Eq. (4), X denotes O, OH, or OOH; E and G denote electronic energy and Gibbs free energy; T is temperature; S is entropy; and ZP identifies the zero point contribution. The supplied adsorption free energies already include these corrections. The four reduction potentials for the associative four electron ORR pathway were evaluated with the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) method [10.1021/jp047349j]:
P00033  			[[MATH: U_{1} = (4.92 eV - ΔG_{OOH}) / e
U_{2} = (ΔG_{OOH} - ΔG_{O}) / e
U_{3} = (ΔG_{O} - ΔG_{OH}) / e
U_{4} = ΔG_{OH} / e]]	(5)
P00034  Equation (5) defines the positive reduction potentials for the four steps O₂ to OOH, OOH to O, O to OH, and OH to H₂O; * identifies an adsorbed intermediate and e is the elementary charge. The limiting potential is defined as the lowest of the four step potentials:
P00035  			[[MATH: U_{L} = min(U_{1}, U_{2}, U_{3}, U_{4})]]	(6)
P00036  The symbol on the left of Eq. (6) denotes the limiting potential. The ORR overpotential was evaluated relative to the equilibrium potential of 1.23 V:
P00037  			[[MATH: η_{ORR} = 1.23 V - U_{L}]]	(7)
P00038  Equation (7) defines the ORR overpotential. Chemical subscripts in Eqs. (5)–(7) identify the adsorbed intermediate or reaction quantity.
P00039  2.4. Scaling and thermodynamic activity analyses
P00040  The relations of O and OOH adsorption free energies to OH adsorption free energy were evaluated by ordinary least squares:
P00041  			[[MATH: ΔG_{O} = α_{O} ΔG_{OH} + β_{O}
ΔG_{OOH} = α_{OOH} ΔG_{OH} + β_{OOH}]]	(8)
P00042  In Eq. (8), α and β are the slope and intercept for the intermediate identified by the subscript. The fitted relations were propagated through the four ORR steps to obtain the scaling based activity curve in Figures 2 and 3. An ideal reference relation was defined with an OOH–OH free energy separation of 2.46 eV:
P00043  			[[MATH: ΔG_{OOH}^{ideal} = ΔG_{OH} + 2.46 eV
η_{ideal} = 1.23 V - min(ΔG_{OH} / e, (2.46 eV - ΔG_{OH}) / e)]]	(9)
P00044  In Eq. (9), OH removal defines the left branch and OOH formation defines the right branch. The branches meet at an OH adsorption free energy of 1.23 eV and zero overpotential. Catalyst activity was interpreted thermodynamically; kinetic activity also depends on activation barriers.
P00045  2.5. Regression analysis and catalyst-family screening
P00046  The DFT adsorption results for the molecular catalyst library supplied the scientific basis for regression analysis. The separate clean catalyst database contains 1176 optimized structures; these structures were calculated with GPAW 25.7 and ASE 3.28.0 using the other settings reported in Section 2.2. Figure 3 reproduces the supplied fixed ridge models for OH, O, and OOH adsorption free energies. Eight predictors were used for the OH and O models, and six were used for OOH. The saved validation predictions were reproduced for 69 observations, with a maximum difference of 1.11 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV. The 1176 structures define a separate screening library, and their adsorption predictions are not represented by Figure 3.
P00047  The predicted values were joined by catalyst name and `ads_site` before the CHE step potentials and overpotentials were calculated. The energy filters used for the DFT analysis were then applied. All 144 filtered site pairs from 87 catalysts are shown in Figure 3, including records used to fit the supplied models. The plot therefore describes the represented molecular set and its fitted relations.
P00048  2.6. LLM manuscript generation
P00049  A large language model (LLM) accessed through OpenAI Codex was used to prepare the manuscript from the scientific inputs. Data to paper generation with interacting LLM agents and source linked outputs has been reported by Ifargan and colleagues [10.1056/aioa2400555]. The present procedure was designed to generate a complete article from a consolidated prompt after human guided development.
P00050  The manuscript instructions were developed through repeated human guided iterations over approximately two weeks. Scientific questions, databases, analyses, figures, literature, journal requirements, terminology, and writing guidance were supplied to the LLM. Ten defined functions were assigned to project curation, scientific auditing, programming, figure preparation, supervision, drafting, literature review, document construction, style editing, and release auditing. Scientific, terminological, structural, and stylistic corrections were incorporated into the project instructions.
P00051  The complete article was generated by applying the consolidated prompt to the verified scientific inputs and tested instructions. The resulting text was checked against the calculations, predictions, figures, literature, and data records by the human researchers, who approved the scientific account.

P00058  3.2. Adsorption scaling and calculated ORR thermodynamics
P00059  Figure 2a shows the O and OOH adsorption free energies as functions of OH adsorption free energy. The OOH–OH relation has a slope of 0.69 and intercept of 2.98 eV, while the O–OH relation has a slope of 1.66 and intercept of 1.20 eV. These relations describe two different balances: OOH binding tracks OH binding more closely, whereas O varies more strongly across the sampled structures. The two relations then determine how adsorption thermodynamics propagates through the four ORR steps.

P00063  3.3. Regression predictions across catalyst families
P00064  The regression predictions extend the comparison across the molecular structures represented by the supplied models. Among the 144 filtered catalyst and adsorption-center pairs, the mean predicted ORR overpotential is lowest for Fe adsorption centers (0.40 V) and next lowest for Co adsorption centers (0.65 V). Mn, Cr, Ni, and Cu groups have higher means of 0.73, 1.04, 1.10, and 1.42 V, respectively. The model predicts adsorption free energies; the ORR overpotentials are calculated from those predictions through the CHE step potentials. The family-level pattern identifies Fe and Co centers as useful priorities for further catalyst investigation.
P00065  The three lowest predicted overpotentials among the 144 retained catalyst and adsorption-center pairs were 0.30 V for a Mn–Fe DASC with an S bridge, B1 diamine, and Fe adsorption center; 0.31 V for a Cr–Fe DASC with the same bridge, diamine, and adsorption center; and 0.31 V for an Fe–Co DASC with the same structural features. These are regression-derived estimates, not additional DFT calculations. These predictions are conditional on the +2 total charge and spin treatment described in Section 2.2. The predicted family pattern complements the DFT results. Explicit calculations establish how the sampled molecular structures bind O, OH, and OOH, while regression estimates the same adsorption quantities across represented structures without a separate adsorption calculation for each point. The available predictions therefore help narrow molecular choices for the next scientific question. The 1176-structure clean database defines a distinct target set whose adsorption predictions are outside the present Figure 3 results.

P00068  3.4. Alternative activity descriptors and reusable DFT data
P00069  The same site matched adsorption data support more than one thermodynamic reading of ORR. The conventional CHE overpotential identifies the least favorable individual reaction step. Calle-Vallejo's electrochemical step symmetry index (ESSI) instead summarizes how evenly the four step potentials share the 1.23 V reaction free energy, while Exner's free energy span identifies the largest cumulative uphill change across consecutive reaction states at a selected potential [10.1016/j.coelec.2018.03.025; 10.1021/acscatal.2c03997]. These descriptors ask different questions of the same adsorption energetics. Their calculation from one verified dataset illustrates why molecular identity and adsorption-center identity should remain attached to every value.
P00070  The parent molecular catalyst dataset is available as ASE and JSON records in Zenodo [10.5281/zenodo.18601379]. The five adsorption databases used for the DFT analysis and the analysis code will accompany the final data release. The separate bare catalyst database contains 1176 optimized molecular structures and is available at the project repository. Each adsorption record retains its catalyst and adsorption-center identifiers, which support CHE, ESSI, and free energy span analyses from the same molecular data.
P00071  3.5. Closing the scientific communication gap
P00072  The catalyst study gives the LLM a concrete body of scientific evidence to communicate. DFT calculations establish the adsorption record, and regression predictions expose family level patterns. The complete article joins those results to the relevant literature and explains their chemical meaning. Scientific communication thus becomes the third stage of the research itself: it carries verified knowledge to the researchers who will use it.
P00073  Over approximately two weeks, we taught the LLM the project terminology, scientific methods, source hierarchy, journal requirements, figure rules, and manuscript voice. We tested its drafts against the ASE records, calculations, regression outputs, literature, and our scientific interpretation. That preparation produced a consolidated prompt that generated the complete article. The development required sustained human judgment; the final prompt gathered that judgment into a repeatable writing procedure.
P00074  We chose the research questions, performed the DFT calculations, selected and checked the regression analysis, and decided what the results support. The LLM organized and wrote the article from those verified inputs. This division gives researchers a practical way to hand off prose while keeping the scientific method in their hands. It also lets the same structured data support analyses that the original manuscript did not need to anticipate.
