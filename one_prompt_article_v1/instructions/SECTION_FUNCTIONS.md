# Sentence functions for a research article

## Scope and freedom

This reusable guide specifies the work a sentence should do, not its wording. Obtain the research question, contribution hierarchy, verified results, terminology, voice, and journal requirements from the explicit input package. Combine or split functions when the argument benefits; sentence counts are planning aids, not quotas. Never turn these instructions into fill-in sentences or copy an earlier manuscript.

When the article packet supplies a paragraph-and-sentence plan, treat each slot as a rhetorical job filled with its supplied facts and evidence. Preserve the intended progression and contribution hierarchy while choosing original wording and natural sentence boundaries. Templates specify functions, not prewritten sentences. Check the filled paragraph for continuity; omit a closing summary when the preceding sentences already complete its purpose.

## Abstract: one argument in approximately six to eight sentences

1. Establish the scientific subject and specific challenge, using the title's central terms. Avoid a universal declaration about the importance of science.
2. State the question or contribution that addresses that challenge, connected causally to sentence 1.
3. Identify the study design and evidence needed to evaluate the contribution. Include sample size only when it establishes scientific scope.
4. State the principal verified scientific result and its meaning, rather than every numerical output.
5. Explain how a subsequent analysis advances the investigation, if present. Distinguish calculations from statistical predictions.
6. State the principal achievement and its evidence. For a methods demonstration, this can be the demonstrated procedure rather than the best specimen.
7. Resolve the opening challenge with a bounded implication. End with what the study enables, not administrative obligations or unsupported superiority.

A multi-stage study may need a connecting sentence before its principal achievement. Keep one clear contribution hierarchy. Define necessary abbreviations under the selected policy. Exclude citations unless essential and journal-permitted, implementation tolerances, database identifiers, and future work presented as a result. Apply the journal word limit after the argument is coherent.

## Introduction

Opening paragraph: sentence 1 identifies the title's scientific problem concretely; sentence 2 explains the process that creates it; sentence 3 supplies relevant evidence; the last sentence identifies the consequence motivating this study.

State-of-the-art paragraph: sentence 1 states what an established method or position accomplishes; subsequent sentences explain relevant findings with citations; the final sentence identifies the unresolved question. Attribute editorial opinions as opinions. Never strengthen a source's position merely to create opposition.

Motivation paragraph: sentence 1 states the study's thesis; sentence 2 explains why the proposed method can test it; sentence 3 identifies the necessary evidence or conditions; the closing sentence introduces the concrete case.

Final paragraph: sentence 1 states the question investigated; subsequent sentences connect major stages by scientific purpose; the last sentence identifies the contribution. Avoid a miniature Methods section or inventory of forthcoming sections.

## Methods

Within each subsection, sentence 1 identifies the object and procedure; sentence 2 gives input provenance and selection basis; subsequent sentences specify operations and parameters affecting reproducibility; the last sentence defines the output and any assumption needed for interpretation. Use passive voice when the profile requires it. State software versions once. Describe only performed validation, not desirable procedures presented as accomplished work.

Introduce each equation's purpose before display. Define every new symbol afterward, then any necessary condition. Distinguish chemical species, quantities, and units. Number equations consecutively beside the corresponding display.

## Results

Sentence 1 identifies the question answered by the analysis. Sentence 2 states the main pattern and refers to the figure. Sentence 3 supplies minimum quantitative evidence. Sentence 4 explains its physical or statistical meaning. Sentence 5, when needed, states the consequence or a qualification that changes interpretation. Do not recite every plotted point.

Distinguish individual examples, marginal group trends, and interactions. Favorable group averages do not establish favorable behavior for every member. Separate marginal enrichments do not prove a combined design rule. Specify population and denominator for proportions.

## Discussion

Sentence 1 identifies the interpretation at issue. Sentence 2 connects present evidence to a closely related published result. Sentence 3 identifies agreement or difference and its methodological basis. Close with the conclusion this comparison permits. Preserve reaction, catalyst, and model differences.

For a methodological demonstration, distinguish development effort, execution, and independent verification. Report artifacts as evidence; measured acceleration requires a baseline and timing. Reusable data permit additional analyses without implying that these have all been performed.

## Conclusions

Sentence 1 states the principal demonstrated achievement with the title's central terms. The next one or two sentences connect it to the strongest evidence. Then explain what changes in scientific practice. End with a concrete consequence within the evidence. Use the Introduction's terms and argument; introduce no new result, citation, ranking, or undefined abbreviation. Keep secondary findings subordinate.

## Captions

Begin with a brief scientific identification. Explain each panel's quantity and relationship, then symbols, colors, curves, and special points as needed. Include a selection criterion only when its omission would mislead. Exclude export settings, tick formatting, plotting instructions, and repeated Methods. Include source citations when attribution is required by the article instructions.

## Editorial test

For every sentence ask what it adds, which preceding sentence it develops, and what evidence supports it. Remove orphan declarations, repetition, synonym rotation, ungrounded comparisons, rhetorical filler, and lists disguised by semicolons. Preserve scientifically necessary qualifications. Recheck the paragraph after every local edit.
